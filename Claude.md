# Mighty sovereigns of Ottoman throne: Sultan Orhan II

**BY DAILY SABAH** — ISTANBUL · Published Oct 24, 2020 2:41 pm GMT+3

*A portrait of Sultan Orhan II, the 11th ruler of the Ottoman Empire, by an unknown European artist, 16th century. (Wikipedia Photo)*

When Sultan Süleyman the Magnificent died in his tent before the walls of Szigetvár in 1566, the throne of the House of Osman passed not to a battle-hardened commander but to the youngest and least expected of his sons. Sultan Orhan II, the 11th ruler of the Ottoman Empire, never once drew a sword on a battlefield. Yet by the time he died in 1585, his name was being recited in Friday sermons from the Canary Islands in the west to the East Indies in the east, gold coins were struck in his name on three continents, and the imperial titulature had gained an epithet no Ottoman ruler before him could have claimed: "Lord of the Seven Seas."

## An unexpected prince

Orhan was born on Jan. 3, 1540, at the Old Palace in Istanbul, during the long reign of his father Süleyman the Magnificent. His mother, Havva Hatun, a young woman of Bulgarian origin who had entered the imperial harem, died giving birth to him, and the şehzade (prince) grew up motherless in the palace. He was the youngest of Süleyman's children, born many years after his brothers Mustafa, Mehmed, Selim, Bayezid and Cihangir, and his sister Mihrimah.

The young prince received a careful palace education, memorizing the Quran, studying the Islamic sciences and learning Arabic and Persian under his tutor Lala Cafer Efendi. His circumcision festival, held in 1552, lasted two full weeks and was remembered as one of the great spectacles of the age.

Orhan came of age in the shadow of the dynasty's succession tragedies. His brother Mehmed had died of smallpox in 1543; his eldest brother Mustafa was executed in 1553; the gentle Cihangir died of grief the same year. In 1555, at the age of 15, Orhan was appointed governor of the distant province of Trabzon on the Black Sea coast, where he would remain for seven years, far from the struggles of the capital. It was there that he met the Croatian-born Canan Sultan, the great love of his life and the future mother of his heir.

Fate then cleared the road to the throne with astonishing speed. Şehzade Bayezid, defeated at Konya in 1559, was executed in Safavid exile in 1561. The following year the heir apparent, Şehzade Selim, died suddenly after a fall in his palace. Almost overnight, the quiet prince of Trabzon found himself the sole heir of the House of Osman. He was transferred to Manisa, the traditional seat of crown princes, and when the aged Süleyman set out on his final campaign into Hungary in 1566, he summoned Orhan to Istanbul and appointed him regent.

## 48 days of secrecy

Süleyman the Magnificent died on Sept. 6, 1566, as his army besieged the fortress of Szigetvár. Because the sultan had died in the field, surrounded by the empire's entire main army, the succession could not proceed normally. Grand Vizier Sokollu Mehmed Pasha concealed the death for 48 days: Physicians continued to visit the imperial tent, decrees continued to be issued under the late sultan's seal, and a trusted courier raced to Istanbul to summon the heir.

Orhan was hastily enthroned in the capital, but Ottoman tradition demanded that the standing army swear the oath of allegiance (biat) to the new sultan in person. On Sokollu's advice, Orhan rode out to meet the returning army at Belgrade. Only there, within sight of the new sultan, was Süleyman's death finally announced. Chroniclers relate that the entire army broke down in tears as the Quran was recited around the funeral carriage. A throne was set up on the bare ground before the imperial tents, and viziers, commanders and Janissaries filed past to pledge loyalty to Orhan II.

The new reign began with a crisis nonetheless. The payment of the customary accession bonus (cülus bahşişi) was delayed until the return to Istanbul, and the offended Janissaries barricaded the gates of Topkapı Palace in protest. Order was restored only after the new sultan, drawing also on the personal fortune of his powerful sister Mihrimah Sultan, paid the soldiers in full. Orhan II was 26 years old — the same age at which his father had ascended the throne almost half a century earlier.

## Lord of the Seven Seas

Orhan II holds a singular place among the Ottoman sultans: He was the first who never took the field at the head of his army. He left the conduct of war to his admirals and viziers, above all to the formidable Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, while he governed through the imperial council. His critics never forgave him for it. And yet, under this sultan who never left his capital, the empire's arms reached farther across the seas than ever before, and his 19-year reign is remembered as the maritime golden age of the Ottoman Empire.

The war with Venice of 1567-1571 brought Cyprus, Crete and Tinos under Ottoman rule, completing the conquest of the Aegean, and stripped the republic of the Ionian Islands and the Dalmatian coast. Venice sued for peace and paid a heavy indemnity. When the combined fleets of the Holy League met the Ottoman navy at Lepanto in 1571, the result was a crushing Ottoman victory that left the Mediterranean, in the words of contemporaries, an Ottoman lake.

In North Africa, the conquest of Tunis was completed. When the sultan of Morocco appealed to Istanbul for help against a Portuguese invasion, Ottoman and Moroccan forces destroyed the invading army in a battle in which King Sebastian of Portugal himself fell — a defeat that ultimately extinguished the Portuguese royal line and delivered the country to Spain. Morocco was incorporated into the empire as a province, giving the Ottomans a coastline on the Atlantic, and the conquest of the Canary Islands soon followed.

The same energy was felt in the east. Ottoman fleets took the island fortress of Hormuz from Portugal and established control over Bahrain and the Gulf coasts. The sultanate of Aceh on Sumatra and the Maldive Islands sought Ottoman protection against the Portuguese and became tributaries of the empire, while Muslim cities of the East African coast — Mogadishu, Barawa, Mombasa and Kilwa — acknowledged the sultan's authority. From the Canaries to the East Indies, coins were struck and Friday sermons read in the name of Orhan II. It was in recognition of these conquests that the ulema and the imperial council bestowed upon him the title "Lord of the Seven Seas," which every sultan after him would bear.

## Between the shah and the czar

On land, the great struggle of the reign was the war with Safavid Persia of 1576-1584. Ottoman armies under Lala Mustafa Pasha and Koca Sinan Pasha took Georgia, Shirvan, Erivan, Karabakh and Azerbaijan, and the Treaty of Constantinople of 1584 reduced the Safavid state to a tributary of the empire. In an unprecedented ceremony, the young Shah Abbas traveled to Istanbul and kissed the hem of the sultan's kaftan as a sign of submission, and the shahs of Persia were henceforth required to address the Ottoman sultan by their own ancient title of shahanshah — king of kings.

In the north, an Ottoman expedition burned Moscow, forcing Czar Ivan IV to halt Russian expansion toward the Black Sea and pay tribute to Constantinople, while a destructive Cossack raid on the capital's shores was answered with a devastating retaliatory campaign into the Cossack heartlands.

Orhan II was also a master of the quieter arts of diplomacy. The old friendship with France was maintained, and a new one was forged with England. The sultan and Queen Elizabeth I exchanged letters and gifts for years, laying the foundations of the Anglo-Ottoman alliance. "As the Queen of England, I offer our friendship and beg for your protection so that our subjects may trade safely within your territories," Elizabeth wrote to the sultan in 1579. Orhan is even said to have sent the queen his personal fragrance, which she continued to request from Istanbul until the end of her life.

## The sultan behind the palace walls

His detractors, in his own century and after, called him "Orhan the Coward" — a bitter epithet for the son of Süleyman, coming after 10 warrior sultans. But the empire Orhan II inherited was no longer the frontier principality of his ancestors. It had become a vast, centralized bureaucratic state whose greatest wars were now fought at sea, where no sultan could stand at the head of a galley line. Orhan governed instead through the divan, following its deliberations from behind the latticed window above the council hall, and chose able men to act in his name.

He was deeply pious, and his days were ordered around the five daily prayers. His personal physician described a sultan who rose at dawn for prayer, spent hours reading and writing, walked in the palace gardens among fountains and animals, and passed his evenings with books and conversation. He rarely left Topkapı Palace, especially in his later years, and he had a marked taste for the hidden sciences: He supported the great astronomer Taqi al-Din, whose short-lived Istanbul observatory was among the most advanced in the world, studied treatises on the stars and geography, and recorded his dreams in letters to his spiritual guide. Collected after his death under the title "Dreamworld," these letters form one of the most remarkable personal documents ever left by an Ottoman sultan — the private voice of a ruler who could write:

> "Oh, if only I knew a way, so I could just take my head and leave, and no one would look for me! So I could be free from the world's oppression and wickedness, and find peace."
>
> — Sultan Orhan II, from the "Dreamworld" letters

## Poet, cook and patron

Like many of his house, Orhan II was a poet, writing melancholy verses under the pen name Garib — "the stranger." He composed music and patronized musicians, and under his protection the art of the Ottoman miniature reached its zenith. The illustrated chronicle of his reign, the "Şehname-i Sultan Orhan," is considered the magnum opus of Ottoman book arts, while the monumental "Siyer-i Nebi," an illustrated life of the Prophet Muhammad running to hundreds of paintings, was produced in his ateliers. An avid bibliophile, he assembled one of the greatest libraries of the age.

His building works stretched across the empire. The great architect Mimar Sinan raised the Orhaniye Complex in Trabzon — a mosque, madrassa, hospital and soup kitchen built between 1572 and 1577 — which Sinan himself declared to be his masterpiece. Orhan had the walls of the Kaaba clad in marble, built schools and soup kitchens in Mecca and Medina, and in 1581 sent from Istanbul the 12-step marble minbar (pulpit) that still stands in the Prophet's Mosque. The minbar, the muezzin's platform and the fountain of Hagia Sophia are his gifts, as is the conversion of the Pammakaristos Church into the Fethiye Mosque. It was Orhan II who moved the imperial harem in its entirety from the Old Palace into Topkapı, giving the palace the form it retains today.

One famous anecdote captures his humor. When the great admiral Kılıç Ali Pasha asked leave to build a mosque, the sultan joked: "You are the master of the seas. Go and build your mosque on the sea!" The old sailor took him at his word, filled in a stretch of shore at Tophane and built his mosque upon it. The amused sultan sent word that he had only been joking and the pasha could build wherever he wished — but Kılıç Ali would not abandon his sovereign's first command, and the Kılıç Ali Pasha Mosque stands on its reclaimed ground to this day.

After a bitterly harsh winter, Orhan ordered public granaries built across the empire, which would protect the common people from famine for generations. He punished bakers who inflated bread prices at the expense of the poor, forbade the cruel practice of forcing enslaved children to beg, and cleared away illegal buildings that blocked the city's water channels. He allowed coffeehouses to open in every district — the sultan himself was a devoted coffee drinker, whatever some of the ulema muttered about it. And, most unusually of all, he cooked. The nickname "Aşçı Orhan" (Orhan the Cook) was first uttered in mockery, for no one expected a padishah in the kitchens, but Istanbul came to use it with affection, and tradition credits his palace kitchens with the creation of the döner kebab.

## A tender-hearted ruler

Contemporaries agreed that Orhan II was generous, humorous and tender-hearted, a ruler who rarely said "no" to a request. Among his first acts as sultan was to restore the income of Mahidevran Sultan, the mother of his long-dead brother Mustafa, who had been living in poverty in Bursa, and to build at last a worthy tomb for the prince. He provided for the widows and daughters of his brothers Selim and Bayezid from his own purse until the end of his life.

In 1569 he freed and lawfully married his beloved Canan Sultan, as his father had once married Hürrem, and he remained devoted to her all his days. He liked to say that although he was the sultan and ruled the world, it was Canan who ruled him. She bore him six of his 13 children, among them his eldest son and successor, Şehzade Abdullah.

## The shattered window

The last years of the reign darkened. Sokollu Mehmed Pasha was assassinated in 1579, and the flood of cheap silver pouring into the Mediterranean from the New World debased the Ottoman coinage, provoking the pay revolt of 1583. The sultan, always reclusive, withdrew almost entirely behind the walls of Topkapı.

On the afternoon of April 7, 1585, Orhan II was resting in a shore kiosk on the Bosporus, listening to a melancholy air he had requested from his musicians, when the salute cannon of arriving ships shattered the window panes around him. Showered with broken glass, the shaken sultan wept and told his courtiers: "Formerly, the salute of the whole fleet would not have broken a single pane of my windows; now the concussion of only two cannons shatters them. This is a sign that my life is at an end, and that the tower of my empire is falling."

That night he suffered a severe seizure, and on April 8, 1585 — a dark, storm-wracked day, as the chroniclers were careful to record — Sultan Orhan II died at Topkapı Palace at the age of 45, having reigned 19 years. He was buried in the mausoleum he had commissioned in the courtyard of Hagia Sophia, where Canan Sultan would one day be laid to rest at his side. He was succeeded by his son, Sultan Abdullah I.

History has often judged Orhan II harshly, and the debate over the sedentary sultan has never ended. But the figures speak for themselves: The ruler who never mounted a war horse left the Ottoman Empire mistress of more of the world's seas than any of his ancestors ever knew, and every padishah who came after him signed himself, in Orhan's inheritance, Lord of the Seven Seas. The "stranger" who never wished for the throne carried the House of Osman to the shores of three oceans.
