# Gazel

*attributed (fictionally) to Muhibbî — Sultan Süleymân, called the Lawgiver*
*redif: "again"*

---

The rose comes back to the same bough; the world is green and grown again.
The cage stands open as I left it — my falcon is not flown again.

Ask why the gardener weeps beside a pruning-hook so bright:
the bough he cut to spare the tree will not be his own again.

Fill it, sâkî. Wine was once the fire that ate my grief;
now the cup is Cem's own mirror, and one face is shown again.

They carried a shirt to Ya'qûb once, with blood that was not his son's,
and the old man believed the cloth, and both his eyes were stone again.

I trusted a line of writing more than a face I knew by heart.
Now every seal I break is false, and no hand is known again.

They say the belovèd's curl is a noose the neck is glad to wear.
One knot of silk at the dark of the moon — and the throne was mine alone again.

A candle guttered in my house; the wind was not yet finished:
before the almond flowered, a second wick was blown again.

Let them write that the world is mine from Buda to the Tigris —
there is no kingdom like a breath a poor man draws, unknown, again.

When the scrolls fly open I will have no argument to make;
I will beg the Chosen One for shade — a name I cannot own again.

Muhibbî — "the one who loves" — is the name you sign beneath the page.
Süleymân, you pressed the seal, the wax went hard: the word is not your own again.

## Notes on the craft

**Why a gazel.** Muhibbî left roughly 2,799 of them. The form is fixed: the opening
couplet (*matla*) rhymes both halves, every later couplet rhymes only its second half,
the couplets are semantically free-standing rather than argued in sequence, and the poet
names himself in the last couplet (*mahlas*). Nothing here is stated; everything is
placed beside something else. That is the machinery that lets a sultan write a confession
that a court reader could hear as a love poem.

**The redif.** Muhibbî favours a repeated tail phrase — "hoş gelir," "gibi," "oğul."
"Again" was chosen because regret is a repetition: the world's returns (spring, roses,
the turning wheel) set against the one thing that does not return.

**What the images are carrying.**

- *The falcon and the open cage* — the prince kept in his province, the empty hunt.
- *The gardener who cuts the bough to spare the tree* — the exact argument made in 1553:
  a prince too beloved by the army must be removed for the dynasty's safety. Muhibbî
  gives it to a gardener, and lets the gardener weep.
- *Cem's cup* (the world-revealing goblet of Jamshid) — the standard wine trope,
  turned into a mirror that will only ever show one face.
- *Ya'qûb and the shirt* — the Qur'anic father deceived by his sons with false blood on
  a garment. The correspondence to the forged letters bearing a prince's seal is exact,
  and completely deniable: it is scripture, not court gossip.
- *The tress as a noose* — the most conventional image in the divan (the beloved's curl
  is a lasso the lover longs to be caught in). Here "one knot of silk" is the bowstring,
  and the couplet's second half admits what it bought.
- *The second wick* — Cihangir, who did not survive his brother's death by long.
- *"There is no kingdom like a breath"* — a direct answer to his own famous couplet:
  "there is nothing esteemed among men like sovereignty / there is no sovereignty in the
  world like one breath of health."
- *The Chosen One* — on its face, the Prophet, whose intercession a Muslim begs on the
  Day of Gathering; entirely orthodox, entirely unremarkable. *Muṣṭafâ* means "the
  chosen." It is the only time the name appears, and it appears as a prayer.
- *The seal* — Süleymân is Solomon, whose ring commanded the world. The poem ends on the
  one true mechanism of the regret: an order, once sealed, stops belonging to the man who
  sealed it.

**On the fiction.** No such poem exists. Süleymân's remorse is well attested — the
dismissal of Rüstem Pasha, the public mourning, the ambassadors' reports — but he never
wrote Mustafa an elegy. The elegy that survives is Taşlıcalı Yahya's, and it cost its
author his place at court. This poem is an invention of what the other side of that
silence might have sounded like.
