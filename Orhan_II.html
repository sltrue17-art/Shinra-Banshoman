Orhan II (Ottoman Turkish: اورخان ثانی, romanized: Orhan-ı sānī; Turkish: II. Orhan; 3 January 1540 – 8 April 1585) was the eleventh sultan of the Ottoman Empire, reigning from 1566 until his death in 1585. The youngest surviving son of Suleiman I ("the Magnificent"), he came to the throne after the deaths of his elder half-brothers and is remembered chiefly for the maritime expansion of the empire during his reign, for the cultural patronage of his court, and for his secluded, palace-bound manner of rule.

Orhan never personally led an army on campaign—the first Ottoman sovereign of whom this is recorded—and delegated military affairs to his viziers, foremost among them the grand vizier Sokollu Mehmed Pasha. Under his reign Ottoman fleets and armies brought Cyprus, Crete, the remaining Aegean islands, much of the North African coast and the shores of the Persian Gulf and Red Sea under Ottoman authority, and Ottoman influence was extended into the Indian Ocean. In recognition of these conquests he was given the honorific Pâdişâh-ı Heft Deniz ("Lord of the Seven Seas"). He was also a poet, composer and noted cook, and was known in later tradition by the epithets Aşçı ("the Cook") and, more enduringly, Zayıf ("the Weak").

Early life

Orhan was born on 3 January 1540 at the Old Palace (Eski Saray) in Constantinople, the youngest child of Sultan Suleiman I. His mother, Havva Hatun, was a concubine of Rumelian Christian origin, said to have come from a Bulgarian family; little is recorded of her, and she died in or shortly after childbirth.[1] By the time of his birth Suleiman's surviving children were already grown or near adulthood, and Orhan was raised in the imperial harem without a mother.

He received the customary princely education, learning Arabic and Persian and the Islamic religious sciences, and was circumcised in 1552 in a festival said to have lasted two weeks.[2] The execution of his eldest half-brother Şehzade Mustafa in 1553, and the deaths in the following decade of his half-brothers Şehzade Bayezid (1561) and Şehzade Selim (1562), progressively narrowed the line of succession. In 1555 Suleiman appointed Orhan provincial governor (sancakbeyi) of Trebizond, where he served until 1562; his administration there is generally regarded by contemporaries as undistinguished. After becoming heir apparent he was transferred to the governorship of Manisa, and on his father's departure for his final campaign he was summoned to the capital and left as regent.

Accession

Death of Suleiman and the march from Szigetvár

Suleiman I died on the night of 6–7 September 1566 during the siege of Szigetvár in Hungary, while the main imperial army was in the field far from the capital. To prevent unrest among the troops, the grand vizier Sokollu Mehmed Pasha concealed the sultan's death for several weeks, having physicians attend the imperial tent and issuing decrees under Suleiman's seal while a courier was dispatched to Orhan.[3] A preliminary enthronement was held in Constantinople on 29 September 1566, but it was not regarded as binding by the army, whose oath of allegiance (biat) was held to be necessary for full legitimacy.

Oath at Belgrade and the janissary revolt

Sokollu marched the army southward, the soldiers reportedly still believing the late sultan to be alive in his litter. The death was announced only when the army reached Belgrade, where Orhan awaited it; an imperial throne was set up outside the camp and the assembled commanders, viziers and janissaries swore allegiance to the new sultan.[3] Orhan, however, withheld the customary accession donative, promising payment on the army's return to the capital. The resentment this caused contributed to a violent janissary revolt in Constantinople, during which the troops barricaded the palace gates until Orhan apologised and paid the bonus. His accession was widely regarded as illegitimate by the army, the bureaucracy and the public, a hostility that shaped the early years of his reign.[4]

Reign

Maritime expansion

The reign of Orhan II is conventionally described as a maritime golden age of the Ottoman Empire. Ottoman fleets completed the conquest of Cyprus and Crete and of the remaining Aegean islands, secured the islands of the Red Sea together with Socotra, and established Ottoman authority along the Arabian shores of the Persian Gulf, including Bahrain, Lahsa and the coasts of present-day Qatar, Oman and the lower Gulf.[5] In the western Mediterranean the regency of Tunis was fully incorporated into the empire, and Ottoman forces went on to take the Canary Islands. Attempts on Sicily, Malta and the Balearic Islands failed, as did expeditions directed toward the Atlantic, where the naval power of the joined crowns of Spain and Portugal remained the empire's principal adversary.

In 1578 the Ottomans intervened in Morocco at the request of the Saadi ruler against a Portuguese invasion; the Portuguese king was killed in the campaign, and Morocco was annexed as an Ottoman province, giving the empire a gateway to the Atlantic.[5] In the Indian Ocean the empire contested the Strait of Hormuz with the Portuguese and received the submission of states along the Swahili coast; Ottoman authority was acknowledged at Mogadishu, Barawa, Mombasa and Kilwa, while the Sultanate of Aceh and the Maldives entered into tributary relations with the Porte.[6] At its widest extent the sultan's name was read in the Friday sermon and struck on coinage from the Canaries to the East Indies, and it was for these achievements that Orhan received the title Lord of the Seven Seas. The wealth drawn from the Atlantic and Indian Ocean trade filled the imperial treasury, though it also contributed to the monetary instability of his later years.

Government

Orhan delegated the conduct of government largely to the imperial council (Divan) and to Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, who had been appointed grand vizier by Suleiman in 1565 and retained the office under the new sultan. The relationship between the two men was uneasy: Orhan is reported to have distrusted Sokollu's accumulation of offices and the advancement of his clients, and to have protected rival statesmen at court, while Sokollu retained effective control of administration and foreign policy until his assassination in 1579.[7] The sultan was nonetheless an active participant in the politics of his reign rather than a mere figurehead, and the period after Sokollu's death was marked by greater instability.

Orhan's reign also saw a lasting change in the topography of the court. Following his accession he moved the imperial harem permanently from the Old Palace into Topkapı Palace, uniting the sultan's residence and household in a single complex—a development that increased the political prominence of the women of the harem.[8]

Foreign relations

Relations with France continued on the established footing of capitulatory commerce. New ties were formed with the Dutch rebels against Spanish rule and with England: Orhan and Elizabeth I corresponded and exchanged gifts, forming the basis of an Anglo-Ottoman commercial understanding founded in part on shared hostility to Habsburg Spain.[9]

To the most sublime and powerful ruler of the world, the protector and sole sovereign of the Islamic faith… As the Queen of England, I offer our friendship and beg for your protection so that our subjects may trade safely within your territories. — Letter of Elizabeth I to Orhan II, 1582

Relations with the Mughal Empire deteriorated over the Mughals' refusal to recognise the Ottoman caliphate; tensions rose sharply in 1580 when Ottoman officials were said to have slighted members of the household of Emperor Akbar during the Hajj, causing a temporary suspension of Mughal pilgrimage. To the east, the death of Shah Tahmasp I in 1576 was followed in 1578 by the outbreak of war with Safavid Persia, which continued beyond Orhan's death. In the north, a raid by the Crimean Tatars sponsored by the Porte burned Moscow in 1571, checking the expansion of Tsar Ivan IV toward the Black Sea.[10]

Cultural patronage

Orhan was an active patron of architecture and the arts. He commissioned the chief court architect Mimar Sinan to build the Orhaniye complex at Trebizond (1572–1576), comprising a mosque, madrasa, hospital and soup kitchen; he endowed a madrasa, school, zawiya and soup kitchen at Mecca and Medina, had the walls of the Kaaba faced with marble, and in 1581 sent a marble minbar from Constantinople to the Prophet's Mosque.[11]

A poet who wrote under the pen name Garīb, Orhan also composed music and gave particular encouragement to the art of the Ottoman miniature. The principal monument of his patronage is the Şehnâme-i Sultan Orhan II ("Book of the King of Kings"), an illustrated chronicle of his reign commissioned in 1580 and completed after his death, regarded as a high point of Ottoman manuscript painting.[12] He permitted the opening of coffeehouses and a relaxation of restrictions on coffee, tobacco and wine, measures criticised by the ulema. His reputation as a cook—reflected in the epithet Aşçı—is associated in tradition with the creation of the döner kebab during his reign.

Character

Contemporary accounts describe Orhan as devout, generous and of a mild and humorous disposition, but also as deeply anxious and withdrawn. He took an intense interest in astrology, omens and the interpretation of dreams, and is said to have consulted mystics and dream-interpreters before major decisions. In his later years his fear of deposition and assassination led him to withdraw almost entirely within Topkapı Palace; for two successive years he did not attend the Friday procession to the imperial mosque, an unprecedented breach of custom which the historian Mustafa Selaniki attributed to reports of janissary plots.[13]

Much of what is known of his inner life derives from a collection of his private letters to his spiritual guides, circulated after his death under the title Dreamworld, in which he recorded his dreams and religious experiences at length.[14] The physician attending the court left a description of the sultan's daily routine in 1580:

In the morning he rises at dawn to say his prayer… Then he begins to give audience to the members of the Divan on the three days of the week that this occurs. Then he goes for a walk through the garden… Then he stops to pass the time and amuse himself with the cooks of the palace… He never fails to observe this schedule every day. — Report of the sultan's physician, 1580

Sources describe Orhan as slightly built and above average in height, with a light-brown beard, grey eyes and pale complexion, and as scrupulous in matters of cleanliness. Portraits made during and after his lifetime, both Ottoman and European, depict him as of ordinary appearance.

Family

Orhan's principal consort was Canan Sultan (c. 1540–1596), a woman of Croatian origin whom he met during his governorship of Trebizond. He freed her and married her in 1569—an echo of his father's marriage to Hürrem Sultan—and she became his haseki and, in the following reign, valide sultan; she is counted among the most influential women in Ottoman history. A second favoured consort was Feride Sultan (d. 1600), of Bosnian origin.[15]

Orhan had five sons and eight daughters, of whom four died in childhood. His children included Naciye Sultan, the future Abdullah I, Hayat Sultan, Şehzade Ali, Nur Sultan, Esma Sultan, Şehzade Ahmed, Mübeccel Sultan and Aylin Sultan. The eldest son, Abdullah, was appointed governor of Edirne, his brothers Ali and Ahmed to Manisa and Bursa respectively.

Death and succession

Orhan II died at Topkapı Palace on 8 April 1585, aged 45, of a condition later identified as appendicitis.[16] According to tradition, a servant heard him giving the salaam four times on the day before his death and reply that the first caliphs had come to summon him before the Prophet.

His death was concealed for several days by Canan Sultan, who informed only the grand vizier Koca Sinan Pasha and sent for her son Abdullah, that he might reach the capital and be enthroned before his brothers learned of the sultan's death. Abdullah succeeded as Abdullah I, and on his accession ordered the execution of his half-brothers Ali and Ahmed in accordance with the Ottoman practice of fratricide. Orhan was buried in a türbe at Hagia Sophia, where Canan Sultan was later interred beside him at her own request.

Assessment

The historiographical reputation of Orhan II has generally been unfavourable. As a sedentary ruler who never campaigned, succeeding a line of warrior sultans, he was contrasted unfavourably with his predecessors even by contemporaries, and later tradition fixed upon him the epithet the Weak. He has frequently been blamed for the subsequent decline of the empire, in particular for the leniency he showed toward corrupt officials and for the debasement of the coinage in his later years—a response to the influx of New World silver—which provoked the Defterdar revolt of 1583.[17]

More recent assessments note that, despite the unpopularity of his accession and his withdrawal from public life, his reign was for the most part internally stable and saw a substantial expansion of Ottoman territory and influence, and that the characterisation of him as the worst of the Ottoman sultans is an exaggeration.
