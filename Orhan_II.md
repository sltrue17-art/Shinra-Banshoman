# Orhan II

**Orhan II** (Ottoman Turkish: اورخان ثانی, romanized: *Orḫān-ı sānī*; Modern Turkish: *II. Orhan*; 3 January 1540 – 6 April 1585) was the eleventh sultan and third caliph of the Ottoman Empire, reigning from 7 September 1566 until his death on 6 April 1585. The youngest surviving son of Süleyman I "the Lawgiver" (Kanunî) and an obscure concubine called Havva Hatun, his accession was one of the most improbable in the dynasty's history and is generally regarded by contemporaries and later historians alike as a turning point in the character of Ottoman sultanic rule.

His nineteen-year reign coincided with what would later be designated by Ottoman historiographers as the *Devr-i Bahriyye Zerrîn* — the "Golden Maritime Age" — during which the empire's flag was raised from the Açi Sultanate of Sumatra in the east to the *Cezâir-i Hâlidât* (the Canary Islands) in the west, an unprecedented geographical reach summarised in the title formally voted upon Orhan in 1581 by the imperial Divan: **Pâdişâh-ı Heft Deniz**, "Padishah of the Seven Seas." Yet his reputation in his own lifetime, and ever since, has remained one of the most contested in the long catalogue of the House of Osman. The chronicler Mustafa Selaniki, who served much of his career within his court, called him in one passage *"a most clement, devout and lettered prince, who in his soul desired only to be left alone with God and his garden,"* and in another *"a man whose throne was an ember held against his palm, and who reigned with eyes ever turned to the door behind him."*

Almost every contemporary and later writer has agreed upon two things and on almost nothing else: that under Orhan II the empire reached unprecedented wealth and reach, and that the sultan himself appeared to those around him to be the least imperial of the Osmanlı sovereigns since the foundation of the dynasty. The epithets attached to him by both his own subjects and foreign observers — *Aşçı* ("the Cook"), *Garîb* ("the Stranger," his chosen poetic pen-name), and most famously *Zaîf* ("the Weak") — speak to the strange paradox of a reign in which the empire's frontiers grew faster than at any time since Selim I, while its sovereign retreated by degrees into the inner courtyards of the Topkapı until, by his final two years, he was a man almost no one outside the harem and the privy chamber ever saw.

## Background and Family

### Father and Dynasty

Orhan was born during the prime of the reign of his father, Sultan Süleyman I, called *Kanunî* ("the Lawgiver") by his subjects and "the Magnificent" by Latin Christendom. By 1540 Süleyman had reigned for twenty years, conquered Belgrade and Rhodes, broken the kingdom of Hungary at Mohács, besieged Vienna, raided across the Mediterranean with the corsair captains of Algiers, and codified the imperial law into the *kanunnâmes* that bore his name. The empire into which his last son was born stretched from Buda to Basra and from the Crimea to the eastern Maghreb. It was an empire of confidence; the sons born to its sultan that decade entered a world that assumed itself to be in its high noon.

The dynastic question, however, was not so cloudless. Süleyman's queen and legal wife, the Slavic-born Hürrem Sultan — known to Christians as *Roxelana* — had by the late 1530s effected a quiet revolution within the imperial harem. She had been freed from slavery, married to the sultan in a ceremony unprecedented since the early days of the dynasty, and had borne him five children who survived past infancy: Mehmed, Mihrimah, Selim, Bayezid, and Cihangir. Outside her household, only one of Süleyman's other sons had survived to maturity: Şehzade Mustafa, the eldest, born of the earlier favourite Mâhidevrân Sultan. By 1540 Mustafa was twenty-five years old, governor of Manisa, beloved of the army and the people, and universally acknowledged as the heir-apparent.

Within this carefully balanced household — five children of Hürrem in Constantinople, one of Mâhidevrân in the provinces, the consort-mother of the heir living in semi-exile in Bursa — the appearance of a sixth surviving son, by a third woman, was an unwelcome intrusion. That son was Orhan.

### Mother

His mother, known to history only by the Muslim name **Havva Hatun**, is one of those women of the Ottoman harem of whom we know almost nothing certain and almost everything by inference. Contemporary palace registers identify her as *Bulgar asıllı*, of Bulgarian origin, taken from Rumelia in the late 1530s — most probably from the region of Sofia or Filibe (modern Plovdiv) — sold in the slave-market beside the Grand Bazaar of Constantinople, and purchased into the imperial household at an unknown date in 1538 or 1539. A late-seventeenth-century tradition preserved in the *Zübdet-üt Tevârîh* gives her Christian baptismal name as **Teodora**, daughter of a village priest; this is not corroborated by any sixteenth-century source and should be treated with caution, though it has passed into popular memory.

What is certain is that she was very young — the Venetian bailo Daniello Barbarigo, writing in 1541 of palace gossip he had picked up from a Greek dragoman, called her *"una giovinetta bulgara, di non più che sedici anni, di volto chiaro e di capelli oro pallido"* ("a Bulgarian maiden of no more than sixteen, of clear face and pale golden hair"). She entered the harem at the moment when Hürrem's grip upon the sultan's bed was so total that the appearance of any other woman in his chamber had become a remarkable event in itself. The Ottoman writer Mustafa Âlî, looking back from the 1590s, suggested with his customary acerbity that the sultan had been *"persuaded by his physician to take the air of a different garden, lest the same flower wither for too long alone"* — a reference, perhaps, to the seasonal pattern of imperial favour and to medical doctrines of humoural balance that played their own quiet part in harem politics.

Whatever the cause, Süleyman lay with Havva on or about the spring of 1539. She conceived. She bore the sultan's son on 3 January 1540 — and died within hours of the birth.

The official cause given was haemorrhage. *"Allāh dilemiş"* — "God willed it" — wrote the chief black eunuch in his diary, an entry the historian Ahmed Refik reproduced from a now-lost ledger of the harem. But unofficial whispers were heard at once. The senior court physician, the Jewish doctor Moses Hamon, was said in a letter dated some months later to have remarked privately that *"the child came easily, but the mother was less fortunate; her milk had been tasted before her breasts knew it."* Whether this was the careful Latin shorthand of a physician for poisoning, or simply for the corruption of a wet-nurse's milk that had infected the mother, has never been satisfactorily resolved. From the seventeenth century onwards, however, almost every history of Orhan's life took for granted that Havva had been poisoned at Hürrem's instigation, *not to remove the infant — for he was no threat to her sons — but to extinguish the woman who had broken her monopoly on the sultan's bed*. The Venetian dispatches of 1540, when read for what they do not say, are oddly silent on Havva's death; her name is not even given.

The motherless infant was placed with a wet-nurse, the freedwoman Gülbahar, who would remain the closest thing he ever knew to a mother and whose family he provided for richly upon his accession. The dynastic cradle had room for one more child; the harem ledger inscribed beside his name, in the careful hand of the *defterdar*, the single word *Orḫān*.

## Birth and Early Childhood (1540–1552)

### The Name

The name *Orhan* — borne by the second sultan of the dynasty, the conqueror of Bursa and the husband of Theodora of Byzantium — had not been given to any Ottoman prince for more than a hundred years. Its revival in 1540 was remarked upon at once. Some saw it as a happy reaching-back to the founding generation; others as an embarrassed gesture, a way of giving the unexpected child a name dignified enough to honour but obscure enough not to compete with the names of Hürrem's sons (Mehmed, Selim, Bayezid). The Ottoman antiquary Gelibolulu Mustafa Âlî, with his usual eye for omens, would later argue that *"the second Orhan was given the name of the second sultan because, like him, he was to be the husband of foreign women and the father of an unbroken line; in this if in nothing else the choice of the Lawgiver was prophetic."*

### A Sixth Son in a Crowded Generation

The infant Orhan was kept at the Old Palace (*Saray-ı Atîk*), on the third hill of Constantinople — the ancient women's palace where the imperial harem still principally lived, though Hürrem and her immediate household had by then moved into specially built apartments in the Topkapı. Orhan was therefore raised, in his earliest years, at a physical distance from his father. Süleyman saw him on the ceremonial occasions of Bayram, on the family visits to the gardens of Davutpaşa and Üsküdar, and on the great public processions; otherwise the child grew up surrounded by women and eunuchs in a palace which, after Hürrem's departure, had become the residence of the dynastic surplus.

This very obscurity, which the boy resented when he was older, was probably what kept him alive. Hürrem, by every account a careful and far-seeing woman, judged Orhan no immediate threat. Her sons Mehmed and Selim were already men; Bayezid and Cihangir were not far behind. The infant in the Old Palace was, in dynastic terms, a curiosity, not a candidate. As long as his existence did not solidify into a faction, he could be ignored.

Süleyman himself, in the few accounts we have of his behaviour towards the child, appears to have been warm. The Ragusan envoy Nikola Bošković wrote in 1545 of having seen the sultan, then on a hunting return through Üsküdar, pause to dismount and embrace the five-year-old Orhan, *"and he was seen to lift the boy upon his own saddle and to ride with him a short distance, smiling, before delivering him again to his nurse."* The image, however small, was preserved in palace tradition. In private letters of his later years Orhan would write of *"the smell of my father's coat upon a winter morning, and the cold of his ring against my cheek"* as one of the few memories of paternal tenderness that had remained with him.

But the affection had a limit, and that limit was time. Süleyman, ruling at the height of his powers, was constantly upon campaign or at the Divan; he had little to give his youngest son and gave only what he could. Later historians have called him "neglectful" of Orhan, and the word is just, if one understands by it not coldness but absence. Orhan grew up among women, and one of the things he learned earliest, and held longest, was the language of dreams and omens and intercessions — the half-Sufi, half-folk piety of the women of the harem — which would so mark his reign.

### Tutors and Education

By the time he was seven, the boy was placed under the guidance of the *lala*, the princely tutor, in keeping with dynastic custom. His principal *lala* — and the man who would remain his confidant and correspondent for the rest of his life — was **Lala Cafer Efendi**, a Bosnian-born scholar of the Hanafi school, formerly a *müderris* of the Sahn-ı Semân madrasas. To Cafer Efendi we owe almost everything we know of Orhan's inner life as a boy, since Orhan in maturity continued to write to him as he had written as a child, and the *Mecmûa-i Mektûbât* — the collection of these letters, preserved in the Süleymaniye Library — is the foundation of all subsequent biography.

Under Cafer Efendi the young şehzâde learned Arabic to a high standard, Persian to fluency, calligraphy in the *nestâlîk* and *thuluth* hands, the Qur'an by heart by the age of eleven, and the basics of *fıkıh* (jurisprudence) and *kelâm* (theology). His handwriting in later life is small, neat, anxious — *"a hand," the calligrapher Karahisarî's pupil Hasan Çelebî is said to have remarked, "that wishes to take up no more space than it must."* He also received instruction in horsemanship, archery, and the use of the sword, though by every account he was indifferent at all three; his Venetian biographer Costantino Garzoni, writing for the Senate in 1573, would summarise the rumour in a single waspish line: *"di tutte le arti del cavaliere ha quella sola del montare e del discendere"* — *"of all the cavalier's arts he has mastered only those of mounting and dismounting."*

In music and in the arts of the kitchen, however, the boy was precocious. He was given lessons on the *ney* and the *tanbur*; he was permitted to compose, and his earliest verses, three quatrains in Persian written in the hand of Cafer Efendi for his pupil's eighth birthday in 1548, survive. Most strikingly, he was allowed — by the indulgence of the harem stewards — to spend hours in the palace kitchens, watching the *aşçıbaşı* and the apprentice cooks. This passion would remain with him into his sultanate and gave him the affectionate nickname *Aşçı*, "the Cook."

### Circumcision (1552)

The defining public event of his boyhood was his circumcision. By Ottoman custom this was conducted in a great public festival — the *Sûr-ı Hümâyûn*, the imperial festival — to which were summoned not only the princes of the dynasty but also foreign envoys, scholars, dervish orders, and the guilds of the capital. Orhan's *sünnet* was held in the spring of 1552, when he was twelve, in the Hippodrome (*At Meydanı*).

The festival lasted, by the testimony of Selaniki, fifteen days. The Venetian bailo Bernardo Navagero, in his celebrated *relazione* of that year, gave a description: *"On the second day there appeared the procession of the guilds of the city, who passed before the imperial pavilion: the bakers carrying loaves the size of mill-wheels; the goldsmiths bearing a tree of beaten silver whose leaves were of beaten gold; the gunpowder-makers, who set fire to three small mountains of their own making and made the night day; and finally the sugar-confectioners, who had constructed in pastry an entire fortress with seven towers, which afterwards was demolished by the children of the great and divided amongst the poor. The young prince Orhan, sitting beside the Magnificent his father, was observed to laugh much, and to clap his hands, which is a thing the imperial children are not generally permitted to do in public; but the Padishah, in his goodness, did not rebuke him."*

The boy whom Navagero saw clapping his hands beneath his father's eye would, within a single year, learn that he was no longer the curiosity of the Old Palace, but a piece in the great deadly game of dynastic succession. He never clapped his hands in public again.

## The Years of Fear (1553–1555)

### The Execution of Şehzâde Mustafa

In the autumn of 1553, when Orhan was thirteen, news reached Constantinople from the camp at Ereğli that the heir-apparent, Şehzâde Mustafa, had been strangled in his father's tent by the silent executioners of the household. The charge was treason — that Mustafa had been corresponding with the Persian Shah Tahmâsp, was plotting to march on the imperial camp, and had to be removed before he could move. The execution was carried out by the bowstring; the man whose hands actually drew the silk that night was the Croatian-born vizier and damat of the dynasty, **Zâl Mahmud Paşa**, with the assistance of three *dilsiz* (mute) executioners of the privy chamber.

The shock to the empire was deep. Mustafa had been popular among the Janissaries and beloved by the populace. Riots were narrowly averted in the camp; the Grand Vizier Rüstem Paşa, widely suspected of having forged the incriminating letters in collaboration with Hürrem, was dismissed from office to placate the army (he would be reinstated two years later). Şehzâde Cihangir, Hürrem's youngest son, a hunchbacked boy who had loved his older half-brother dearly, fell into a melancholy from which he never emerged and died at Aleppo within a few weeks; his death was officially of grief, and the official account was, in this case, almost certainly true.

The dynastic ledger was redrawn. Of Süleyman's seven surviving sons by Hürrem and the others, two — Mehmed and now Mustafa — were dead by 1543 and 1553 respectively; Cihangir died within weeks. The contenders had narrowed to three: Selim, Bayezid, and the boy at the Old Palace, Orhan. Of these, none of the contemporary observers thought of Orhan as a serious candidate. But the boy himself read the killing of Mustafa not as a distant political event but as a piece of personal news. If Hürrem had reached Mustafa, she could reach him.

### The Letters to Cafer Efendi

The terror of these years is preserved in a sequence of letters Orhan wrote, between thirteen and fifteen, to Lala Cafer Efendi, who by 1553 had been transferred to a *kadılık* in Skopje. There are seventeen of them, none of more than two pages, all of them undated but ordered by internal evidence. They survive in a single bound *cönk* now in the Süleymaniye, and were first published in scholarly form in the late nineteenth century. They are extraordinary documents.

> *"My teacher, I have not slept three nights together. I see in my mind the rope they brought to my brother. I see the way it must have been done — for he was a tall man, and they were short men, and they must have stood upon the cushions to reach his neck. Please, write to me of God's mercy."*

> *"My teacher, I have asked the cook Mehmed Çelebi to taste my food before I take it. He has agreed, for I have promised him my mother's brooch which my father gave me. But what if Mehmed Çelebi himself is bought? What if she has bought him? I have begun to keep a small loaf under my pillow, of bread I have baked myself in the kitchen with my own hands. I sleep with my hand upon it."*

> *"My teacher, last night I dreamt that my brother Selîm came into my room with my brother Bayezid behind him, and they were laughing as though at a feast, and Selîm said: 'Little Orhan, do not run; we have brought you sweet drink.' And I woke with my hand at my throat. Pray for me, my teacher. Pray for me to-night."*

The image of the prince sleeping with a knife under his pillow and a loaf of self-baked bread beneath it — *"so that if I am to be poisoned I shall at least die having eaten my own bread"* — became, in the eyes of nineteenth- and twentieth-century historians, the defining icon of his early life. So too the now-famous arrangement he made with the chief palace cook (eventually Mehmed Çelebi of Bursa, who would follow him to Trabzon and eventually to the throne in Constantinople, becoming under his sultanate the imperial *aşçıbaşı*): every dish that came to Orhan was tasted twice, once at the brazier and once at the table.

It would later be fashionable, even in his own lifetime, to mock this behaviour. Mustafa Âlî, never a friend, wrote in the 1590s that *"the prince in the Old Palace nourished himself on bread and fear in equal portions, and from this diet of fear he was never weaned, even when he sat upon the throne."* It was less often noted, even by Selaniki, that the boy had reason. Two of his half-brothers had died, one of them by the bowstring; his own mother had died in circumstances that the harem itself believed had not been natural; his stepmother held the keys of the empire's most intimate quarters. He was thirteen. He kept his knife under his pillow. He survived.

### The Sanjak of Trabzon

In the autumn of 1555, when Orhan was fifteen, Süleyman appointed him *sancakbeyi* of Trabzon. The province — the remote Black Sea coast, the old empire of the Komnenoi — was a traditional first sanjak for princes of secondary importance: distant from the capital, prestigious enough to honour, peripheral enough to be safe. It was the same province to which Süleyman himself had been sent by Selim I. The appointment was made by a *berât* drawn in the imperial chancery on the second of Şâban 962 AH; it was, in its way, the first signal that the sultan acknowledged Orhan as a man of the dynasty, with a place to take and a household to maintain.

Orhan left Constantinople, by his own admission to Cafer Efendi, *"as a sparrow that has heard the door of its cage open and dares not look behind to see whether it is truly so."* He travelled by sea, with a small household, a tutor, three eunuchs, four pages, and the cook Mehmed Çelebi. He would not return to the capital for eleven years.

## The Trabzon Years (1555–1562)

### A Quiet Governorship

For the better part of seven years Orhan lived in Trabzon. By every contemporary account his governorship was *vasat* — mediocre, middling, undistinguished. He neither distinguished himself by administration nor disgraced himself by misrule. The province was kept in order by his vizier-attendants and by the imperial agents resident in the citadel above the old harbour. The young şehzâde, to the puzzlement of those sent to observe him, devoted himself to gardens, to the *medrese* he founded beside the Çifte Hamam, to the kitchen of his own household, and to the writing of melancholy verse.

> *"In Trabzon I learned three things,"* he would write much later, in a private letter to his physician. *"That the sea has a different colour in every month. That a good pilaf is made with broth and not with water. That a man may live without ambition, but not without fear."*

For the first time in his life he was permitted, in the manner of Ottoman princes, to take consorts. Three were assigned to him from the Old Palace before he left, and others were sent or purchased over the years that followed. The harem of the Trabzon palace was small — Selaniki, who as a young clerk had visited the city in 1559, gives the figure of fourteen women, including servants — but it was a household and not merely a household-in-waiting. In 1558 the first child of that household was born: a daughter, **Naciye Sultan**, to a woman whose name would in a few years be known across the empire.

### Canan

The woman who bore Naciye was at that time still called by the diminutive used among her companions, *Katarinka*. She had been taken — the bailo Andrea Dandolo would write fifteen years later, after extensive enquiries in the Venetian quarter — from a coastal village near Senj, in the Croatian littoral, in the great Uskok raids of the early 1550s; she had passed through the slave markets of Ragusa, of Salonica, and finally of Constantinople, where she had been purchased by an officer of the Old Palace in 1556 and presented, with a number of other girls, to the household of the prince at Trabzon a few months afterwards. She was renamed in the Muslim fashion **Canan**, a name of high poetic register — meaning, in Persian, "the beloved," the object of all love-poetry — which was both a compliment and, in retrospect, a prophecy.

The Venetian Marcantonio Donini, who was permitted an audience with her in 1569, when she was twenty-nine and Haseki Sultan, left the most candid and most reproduced description of her in any Western source:

> *"The lady whom the Padishah loves above all his other women is of an unusual height, taller indeed than many of the men of the court, with limbs strong but not without grace; her hair, when I saw her loose in her own apartment, was of a dark chestnut, falling to below her waist; her eyes are of a colour I have never seen save in the sea south of Cyprus, neither blue nor green, but a true sea-colour; her bosom is most ample; her face, though not in the strictest Italian manner beautiful, has so much fire in it that one cannot remove the eyes from her, and indeed it is reported that the Sultan, when he wishes to be calm, sends his Lady away from him, for he cannot reason in her presence."*

Whether Donini's report is unvarnished observation or part of the literary tradition of Venetian admiration for the *Sultane*, it is at any rate consonant with every other description we have. Canan was a striking woman, physically imposing, with a deep voice and a slow manner of speech in Turkish (which she had acquired only with effort and which she always spoke with a Slavic accent that her enemies mocked and her admirers found charming). What is more important is that, from the very early years at Trabzon, Orhan loved her wholly and would never love any other woman in the way he loved her.

In 1560 she bore him twins — a son, **Abdullah**, and a daughter, **Hayat**. The boy was the first son of his line and, given the steady attrition in the dynastic ranks, a fact of suddenly increased political weight. After the birth Orhan broke a deep custom: he kept Canan beside him, in his own pavilion, when the established rule was that the mother of a son should pass from the bedchamber and become the *valide* of the future prince. He would do so again, more conspicuously, on the throne.

### The Konya War and Its Aftermath

In 1559, the bitter rivalry between Şehzâde Selim, governor of Manisa, and Şehzâde Bayezid, governor of Amasya, came to open war. Their two armies met outside Konya on the great plain south of the city in May. Selim, supplied with Janissaries by his father and supported by the loyal Sokollu Mehmed Paşa (then third vizier), defeated Bayezid in a single afternoon's hard fighting. Bayezid fled eastward with his four sons and the remnants of his household and threw himself upon the mercy of Shah Tahmâsp of Iran.

The news reached Trabzon by sea in the third week of May. Orhan's reaction is undocumented in surviving letters of the period — a curious silence, which has been read both ways. The harsher reading, advanced by Mustafa Âlî and repeated since, is that he was indifferent. The kinder reading, advanced by his physician Cantemir-zâde Hekîm Ali Çelebi and developed by twentieth-century historians, is that he was terrified. With Bayezid fled, the field had narrowed to Selim and Orhan; with Selim victorious and Bayezid disgraced, the field had effectively narrowed to Selim. And Selim was a man neither of patience nor of mercy.

Tradition has preserved an anecdote, told by Mehmed Çelebi the cook many years later when he was an old man and *aşçıbaşı* under the next reign: that the şehzâde Orhan, hearing of Bayezid's flight, took down from a hook in the kitchen a coarse shepherd's *kepenek* — the felt cloak of the Anatolian highland pastoralists — and said to his cook: *"If they come for me, Mehmed, I shall take this and I shall walk to the mountains, and I shall be a shepherd, and they will not find me. Will you come with me?"* The cook, as he told it, replied: *"Lord, the kitchen of a shepherd is poor. I will come if you will let me bring my copper pans."* The two men, both in middle age by then, laughed about it. But in 1559 the boy had meant it.

In September 1561, after a year of diplomatic wrangling, Shah Tahmâsp delivered Bayezid and his four young sons to Süleyman's emissaries at Qazvin in exchange for an indemnity in gold. They were strangled the same evening. The bodies were returned, washed, and buried in Sivas. The line of Hürrem now stood at one man: Selim.

### The Slip on the Marble

In the spring of 1562, news came overland to Trabzon that would change everything. Şehzâde Selim, then thirty-seven, had been in Edirne attending the imperial hunt with the Janissary aghas and the household troops; on the night of the fifth of Şevvâl (the second week of March), returning to his apartments somewhat exalted in spirit — a polite Ottoman euphemism — he had slipped upon the wet marble of an interior gallery, struck his head against the corner of a fountain basin, and died before dawn.

The news passed through the Ottoman world like a cold wind. Süleyman, then sixty-seven, received it at the Topkapı and is said to have sat in his garden for three days without speaking. The Venetian bailo Marino Cavalli reported home: *"The Magnificent has lost the last of the sons of the Russian woman, and his face is as that of a man whose harvest has been burnt before his eyes."*

The question of how Selim came to fall has been raised since the day of his death. Selim drank — that was no secret; "Selim the Sot" (*Mest*) was a nickname pinned to him long before his death by his political enemies and never quite removed. A drunk man may slip on wet marble, and the floor of the apartment where it happened is documented in palace ledgers as having been newly washed by a servant girl that night. Against this is the perfect convenience of the timing. The only man in the empire who benefited from Selim's death — apart from Selim's own son Murad — was Orhan, who was at that moment in Trabzon some twelve hundred kilometres away.

The contemporary judgment was unanimous: there was no evidence Orhan had a hand in it. He had been visibly settling into the life of a provincial governor; he had ostentatiously distanced himself from the *kapıkulu* and the political networks of the capital; he had no agent in Edirne. The marble had been wet; Selim had been drunk; and the line of Hürrem ended in a fountain basin. The popular verdict, in the end, was that this was God's hand: that the *cülûs* of the youngest son had been written in the *Levh-i Mahfûz*, the preserved tablet, and that no human contrivance was required to enforce it.

Modern historians, particularly those of the post-war school, have been less complacent. The argument, never proven, is that the slip on the marble was too convenient by far; that Canan, by 1562 a woman of formidable acumen, had cultivated discreet correspondences in the household of Selim through the offices of her own kinsmen in Rumelia (her brother, the man known later as Hızır Bey, would surface in the Ottoman service some years afterwards); that the timing — when Süleyman was old and Selim was the sole heir of Hürrem — could not have been better contrived if it had been contrived; and that Orhan, in his *Dreamworld* letters, twice circled and twice failed to write down what he knew or suspected of his brother's death. The case is suggestive and cannot be made. The honest verdict of the empire was, and remains, that we do not know.

### The Murder of Şehzâde Murad

What followed, however, is not in doubt. By the laws of the dynasty, the eligible males of the line of Osman after the death of Selim and before the death of Süleyman were four: Süleyman himself; his last surviving son, Orhan; Selim's only son and only heir, **Şehzâde Murad**, then sixteen years old and recently appointed by his grandfather *sancakbeyi* of Akşehir; and Orhan's two-year-old son Abdullah, in Trabzon. The choice of who should succeed Süleyman, by Ottoman tradition, was the sultan's; by Ottoman law, the throne could pass to any agnate descendant in the male line. Murad, on his mother's side a Venetian-born woman of great character known to history as **Nurbânû Sultan**, possessed the friendship of a Constantinopolitan party that still mourned Selim. Orhan, in Trabzon, possessed nothing beyond his own person, his small household, and his infant son.

He acted. The story is known not from rumour but from the prince's own confession — a confession made to no human ear but written, twelve years later, in a letter to one of his Sufi shaykhs and preserved in the *Dreamworld* collection. There he writes:

> *"My shaykh, I tell this thing only that you may pray for my soul. When the news came of my brother Selîm, I knew that my nephew Murad would by the law of God and the law of my fathers stand between my son and the throne. I knew also that if Murad sat upon the throne my son would not see his fifth year. I sent therefore — through hands which I shall not name even to you, for they have repented and gone to God — gifts and promises to certain men of the household of my brother's son. They did the thing for me. May God forgive me; I did not order it of His name, but of my son's."*

The *thing they did* was poison. Within four months of Selim's death — by midsummer 1562 — Şehzâde Murad sickened suddenly at Akşehir, lingered a fortnight, and died. The physicians spoke of a *kara safrâ*, a black bile; the chronicles wrote *vebâ*, plague; modern toxicological assessment, working backwards from the symptoms preserved in the household register, has tended to suggest arsenic delivered slowly through the prince's morning sherbet. The four servants who were nearest to him at table were dismissed shortly afterwards and surfaced in the years following as men of unexplained wealth in the provinces. Two of them were appointed by Orhan, after his accession, to comfortable sinecures in the Anatolian fisc.

Süleyman is recorded by Selaniki as having received the news of Murad's death with an unusual quietness. He had loved his grandson, and he was an old man, and an old man who has buried four of his sons does not easily bury his grandson too. Whether he ever knew, or whether he ever permitted himself to know, that Orhan had had a hand in the boy's death is one of those questions on which the surviving documents are eloquent only in their refusal to speak. What is certain is that within weeks the old sultan summoned Orhan from Trabzon to Manisa, appointed him *sancakbeyi* of the most prestigious of all princely governorships, and acknowledged him publicly as his heir.

The youngest son, the boy of the Old Palace, the half-brother no one had taken seriously — was now, by the death of five elder brothers, the flight of one, the slip of another upon a marble floor, and the silent passage of poison into a sherbet cup at Akşehir, the only heir of the Lawgiver of the House of Osman.

He was twenty-two years old.

## The Heir Apparent (1562–1566)

### Manisa and Constantinople

Manisa, ancient Magnesia ad Sipylum, was by long custom the *taht sancağı*, the "throne-sanjak," from which the next sultan watched the capital and to which the imperial post-roads ran straight. Orhan was installed in its palace in the autumn of 1562, and Canan and the children followed him within months. The breach of protocol by which Canan had stayed with him at Trabzon was now repeated at Manisa, and remarked upon: a Venetian dispatch of 1563 already calls her *"la Sultana sua sposa nominata, che mai si separa da lui."*

He governed Manisa, as he had governed Trabzon, with the same combination of personal placidity and indifference to detail. The day-to-day work was done by his *kethüdâ*, an able Albanian called Hüseyin Ağa; Orhan himself read, gardened, cooked, prayed, and corresponded. He visited the capital each year for Bayram. He attended his father at Süleyman's last great public function, the wedding of the princess Esmâ to a vizier, in 1565. He met, for the first time in a working capacity, the men who would surround him as sultan.

### Sokollu Mehmed Paşa

The most important of those men was already the most important man in the empire. **Sokollu Mehmed Paşa** — born about 1505 in the village of Sokoloviči in eastern Bosnia, taken in the *devşirme* as a boy, risen through the *enderûn* to the offices of admiral, third vizier, second vizier, and now grand vizier — was at the side of Süleyman the Magnificent everywhere the sultan went. He was already a man of sixty when Orhan met him in any real capacity. He was tall, slow-moving, courteous, devout, and so quietly skilled that Süleyman in his last years signed without reading what Sokollu put in front of him.

The two men had a history. Sokollu had been one of Selim's principal supporters in the Konya war of 1559 and had been the architect, with Rüstem, of the diplomatic settlement that had delivered Bayezid to his executioners. When Selim died, Sokollu's faction was suddenly orphaned; when Murad died, it was suddenly without a candidate at all. Sokollu, a pragmatic man, made his peace with the remaining heir. In a series of confidential interviews at Manisa in the winter of 1562–63, conducted, by all later testimony, with Canan present behind the lattice, the elderly grand vizier and the young prince came to an understanding.

The understanding, as remembered in the household tradition, was a promise: Sokollu would deliver the empire to Orhan when the time came; in return, Sokollu would have the grand vizierate of the new reign. The story has been told and retold ever since. What it conveniently omits is the awkward fact that, in 1565, when the elderly Semiz Ali Paşa died of dropsy, Süleyman himself promoted Sokollu Mehmed Paşa to the office of grand vizier. The promise Orhan had made was now a promise to give a man what he already had.

In Ottoman political theory this was no small matter. The grand vizier of a new sultan owed his office to that sultan; it was a personal bond, formalised in the act of receiving the seal. Sokollu, however, came to Orhan with a seal already in his belt, given to him by Süleyman. The two men were never to escape this awkwardness. Sokollu owed Orhan nothing of his office; he had not been raised by him; he had been confirmed, not elevated. For the next thirteen years, the relationship between the new sultan and his grand vizier would be tense, watchful, mutually irritated, and, in the judgment of every contemporary, indispensable to both.

It was at Manisa, too, that Canan first met Sokollu — at her own quiet suggestion, according to the household tradition. She judged him useful, and she judged correctly. She also, in the long run, judged him too independent of her, and her relationship with him soured in the decade that followed. But for the moment they were allies, and the network of her own friendships among the *âlims*, the *müderris*, the eunuchs of the harem, and the wives of the divan ministers began to form around the embryonic court of the future sultan.

### Regency

In April 1566, Süleyman, now seventy-one and afflicted with gout so severe that he could no longer mount a horse without help, ordered the imperial host to assemble at Edirne for what would be his thirteenth and last campaign. The target was the Hungarian fortress-town of Szigetvár, held by the Croatian-Hungarian magnate Nikola Šubić Zrinski. The sultan rode out — or rather was driven, in a litter — at the head of an army of perhaps a hundred thousand men. Before he left he called Orhan to the capital and entrusted to him the *vekâlet*, the regency.

For four months Orhan sat at the seat of empire as the placeholder of his father. The Divan met under his presidency; the *kazaskers* delivered their judgements in his name. By every account he was an attentive, if cautious, regent: he did not initiate, but he did not obstruct; he listened and signed. The *defterdar* Mahmud Çelebi later wrote that *"the şehzâde-regent was punctual, quiet, and asked questions of the lowest clerks as well as of the viziers, which was thought by some courteous and by others timorous."*

It was an apprenticeship. It was also, although Orhan did not know it, the last weeks of his father's life.

## The Death of Süleyman and the Theatre of Succession (1566)

### Szigetvár

On the night of 6–7 September 1566, deep in the marshes of southern Hungary, before the breached walls of Szigetvár, Süleyman the Lawgiver died in his imperial tent. He was seventy-two. The cause was the slow exhaustion of a body that had outlived its own strength; his physicians spoke of a final attack of apoplexy. The fortress fell the next morning; the news of its fall was sent home; the news of the sultan's death was not.

The grand vizier Sokollu Mehmed Paşa, with what later historians have unanimously praised as the coolest political nerve of the sixteenth century, made the decision in the imperial tent that night that the empire was to be told the truth in due course and not before. The army was deep in enemy territory; the troops, particularly the Janissaries, had not received their accession-money for a new sultan and could not be controlled without it; and Orhan was hundreds of miles away in Constantinople with no military backing. To announce the death now was to invite mutiny, anarchy, or invasion.

What followed has gone into Ottoman political folklore as *Sokollu'nun Oyunu*, "Sokollu's Play." For the next forty-eight days the grand vizier conducted a deception unrivalled in the annals of the dynasty.

> *"The physicians,"* wrote Selaniki, *"were ordered to enter the tent at the hours of the prayers as though to bleed the Padishah, and one of them, by the Magnificent's bed, would speak in the Padishah's voice, for he was an actor in his youth and could mimic the sound of any man; and the words of the Magnificent were drafted by the Grand Vizier, sealed with the Magnificent's tughra by the keeper of the seal, and given out as imperial commands. By this means, the army did not know for forty-eight days what the dogs of the camp had smelled on the third morning."*

A trusted *çavuş* of the household, Süleyman Ağa, was dispatched within hours of the death to ride for Constantinople. His journey, which should have taken twelve days, took twenty-six: the Hungarian rains had broken the roads; he was robbed and lost his horse at Plovdiv; he fell ill at Edirne; he reached Constantinople in the second week of October. He was admitted to the regent's apartments in the Topkapı in the middle of the night, fell to his knees, kissed Orhan's slipper, and said the two words that the entire deception of the previous month had been intended to delay: *Pâdişâhım gitti*. "My Padishah is gone."

Orhan wept. He did not sleep. He did not write to Canan, who was at Manisa with the children. He summoned the *kapı ağası*, the chief white eunuch of the gate, and at dawn on the next day, the morning of the twenty-ninth of September 1566 (some sources move the date forward by a day, the discrepancy unresolved), he received, in a small ceremony in the third court of the Topkapı, the *biât* of the city. He was, in the eyes of the law, sultan.

### The Belgrade Theatre

But it was a hollow ceremony. The army did not know. The grand vizier did not yet sanction it. The instructions Orhan received the next week from Belgrade — Sokollu's messenger came by sea, having outpaced the official news — made the matter plain: the *cülûs* in Constantinople was a stopgap; the real *cülûs*, the one that bound the empire, would happen when the army learnt of the death and swore its oath. This had to be done on Ottoman soil, in a defensible position, with the new sultan present, and at a place where the disorder of the announcement could be contained. The place chosen was **Belgrad** — Belgrade — the great river-fortress at the confluence of the Sava and the Danube.

Orhan rode for Belgrade on the third of November with a small household — Canan went with him, in another breach of custom — and a personal guard of two thousand sipahis under the eye of his sword-bearer. He reached the fortress in the second week of December, in the depth of the Hungarian winter. The army, escorting what they still believed to be the litter of their living Padishah, arrived three days later. The grand vizier rode out to meet the new sultan in private. They embraced, by the witness of Selaniki, *"as two men who have been alone too long in different rooms of the same dark house."*

The *theatre* — there is no better word — that took place at the gates of Belgrade on the eighteenth of December 1566 is among the most remarkable scenes in the political history of the dynasty. The army was drawn up in its full marching order before the eastern walls. The imperial tent was pitched outside the gates, with the litter of Süleyman placed within. The grand vizier appeared at the entrance of the tent in his usual robes, white turban, green caftan. He raised a hand. The cry that ordinarily preceded the sultan's appearance — *Pâdişâhım çok yaşa!* — was given. The flap of the tent opened.

And out came, not Süleyman, but a column of palace clerics in black turbans, chanting the *Sûre-i Yâsîn*. Behind them came Orhan, on foot, in a white caftan and a black turban, with his head uncovered to the cold. Behind him came the *sancak-ı şerîf*, the holy standard of the dynasty, carried by the chief of the household. Behind that, on a great litter draped in green, came the body of the Magnificent.

> *"There rose,"* wrote Selaniki, who was present as a junior secretary, *"a sound that I have not heard before nor since; a sound that was neither the cry of an army nor the lament of a mosque, but the sound of a hundred thousand men learning at once that the world they had known for forty-six years had ended in the night. Some fell upon their faces. Some struck their helmets to the ground. Some, I saw with my own eyes, took dust from the earth and rubbed it into their beards. And the Janissaries — the Janissaries did not weep, but stood as stones, and looked at the new Padishah, and made no sound, and said nothing, and did nothing."*

An imperial throne had been brought out of the fortress and set upon the bare earth between the standards. Orhan, with the grand vizier on his right and the *şeyhülislâm* on his left, took his seat. The *biât* was administered: by orders of seniority, every general, every vizier, every *ağa* of the kapıkulu, every *kâdı* of rank, filed past the throne and laid his hand upon the hand of the new sultan and swore loyalty in the words of the *fıkıh*. The ceremony took, by Selaniki's reckoning, the better part of a day and a night.

It was legally complete. It was politically a disaster.

### The Janissary Revolt

The disaster came in the small print. The *cülûs bahşişi* — the accession bonus customarily paid in cash to the Janissary corps upon the swearing of allegiance — was not distributed at Belgrade. Orhan, badly advised by the *defterdar* of the household (who pointed out that the treasury at Belgrade was insufficient to pay the entire corps and that the rest of the army would expect equal treatment), promised the bonus upon the army's return to the capital. The Janissaries accepted the promise with the immobility that had so unnerved Selaniki at the moment of the announcement; they did not refuse the *biât*, but they did not forget the indignity.

The army marched south. Süleyman's body was carried at its head, in the great *kafes* litter, and at every stage of the road the funeral cortege grew with mourners. The cortege reached Constantinople in the first week of February 1567. The body was taken to the Süleymaniye, washed, prepared, and buried in the *türbe* the dead sultan had built behind his mosque. The funeral was attended by the new sultan, who wept openly throughout — *"as a son for his father,"* wrote Selaniki, *"and not as a king for his predecessor."*

But the gates of the Topkapı were already shut against him.

The Janissaries had reached the city the day before the funeral and had refused to disperse. They had encamped, in their full marching array, around the second courtyard. They had barricaded the inner gates. They had sent word to the new sultan, through the agha of the corps, that they would not yield the palace until he was *present in person* to receive their complaints. And, in a deliberate refusal of the conventions by which the army even at its most insolent had previously addressed the sultan, they did not refer to him in their petition by the title of Padishah. They referred to him as *Şehzâde Orhan*.

Orhan, who in the days of his terror as a boy had imagined being assassinated by his half-brothers' agents, now faced, eight days into his sultanate, the literal embodiment of every nightmare of his adolescence: the corps that had killed Bayezid's sons, that had threatened to depose Süleyman over Mustafa, that had broken sultans before, drawn up under arms inside his own palace, refusing to call him by his title.

He went out to them. He did so on the advice of the grand vizier and against, it would seem, the advice of every other member of his household; Canan, by one account, threw herself before his feet at the door of the harem and begged him not to go. He went, dressed not in imperial state but in mourning. He carried no sword. He came into the second courtyard alone — that is, the grand vizier walked behind him at a distance of fifteen paces, and no other man at all — and he stood in front of the Janissary aghas, and he wept.

> *"I am the son of your father's sultan,"* he is recorded to have said. *"I am the brother of your brothers' sultans. I have done you wrong. I owe you the money of my accession. I shall give it to you to-day. Forgive me my fault."*

He paid them. The treasury was opened that afternoon and the bonus disbursed coin by coin — by some accounts, an amount nearly double the *cülûs bahşişi* paid at the accession of Süleyman, the difference made up from his personal household purse. The Janissaries took the money, raised the customary cry of allegiance, and dispersed. The gates of the Topkapı were opened. The new reign began.

It began with the spectacle, never to be forgotten, of an Ottoman sultan weeping before his own Janissaries to be permitted into his own palace, paying them in coin for the right to call himself their Padishah.

> *"From that day,"* wrote the bailo Marcantonio Donini home to the Senate, *"the great Signor and his Slave-army were as a husband and a wife who have shouted at one another upon the wedding-night; they live afterwards in the same house, but neither forgets, and neither again sleeps soundly."*

## The Sword-Girding and the First Year

### Eyüb

The formal *taklîd-i seyf* — the girding of the sword of Osman, by which the new sultan was inaugurated into the line of his ancestors — was conducted with the customary ceremony at the shrine of Eyüb Sultan, outside the land walls, on the seventeenth of February 1567. The sword was placed about Orhan's waist by the shaykh of the Halvetî order, Şeyh Sünbül Sinan's grandson, who had been chosen for the office by the *şeyhülislâm* Ebussuud Efendi, then in his eighty-seventh year and still the towering moral authority of the Ottoman legal system. The procession from the shrine through the gates of the city was watched, by Selaniki's count, by some two hundred thousand people, and the offerings of bread, sweets, and silver coin scattered along the route from the imperial purse were so lavish that the Greek and Jewish quarters of Balat and Hasköy spoke of it for years as the *iyi gün*, the "good day."

Yet beneath the ceremonial good feeling lay a question no one was prepared to articulate openly. The new sultan was a man no one knew. The portraits sent home by the Venetian baili in those first months convey the puzzlement of careful observers. He was tall but thin; he was learned but said little in public; he was visibly devout but conducted no military review; he had not gone to the army in the field, and he had not pacified the Janissaries by his own person but by the sweat of his treasury. The chronicler İbrahim Peçevî, in a famous summary written long afterwards, expressed the disappointment of the Ottoman political class in the terms that would attach to the new sultan for the rest of his life:

> *"He was a prince, the people said, with the manners of a *müderris* and the courage of a *vâiz*; he would build mosques and write verses, and he would not bleed."*

It was, in the long arc of the reign, an unjust judgment — the empire under Orhan would bleed at twenty frontiers — but it was the judgment that took hold. From his accession he was *Zaîf*, the Weak.

### The Settlements of the Household

Orhan moved fast in the first six months of his reign to settle the personal affairs of his household. Canan was elevated to the office of *Haseki Sultan* — the formal title for the chief wife of a reigning sultan — and within two years was formally manumitted from her slave status and married in religious form, by Ebussuud Efendi himself, in a small ceremony in the harem on the seventh of Cemâziye'l-evvel 976 (29 October 1568). This made her, in the eyes of the *şerîat*, the legal wife of the Padishah. The marriage contract specified a *mehir* of twelve thousand sultanis, of which a sum was paid in coin and the remainder was secured by the deed of a freehold property at Galata. The ceremony was modeled, by Orhan's explicit instruction, on the marriage of Süleyman to Hürrem nearly thirty years before. Foreign envoys were not invited.

The other consorts of the household were settled with their establishments. Saliha Hatun, who had borne Şehzâde Ali in 1563, had died in childbirth, in circumstances harrowingly reminiscent of his own mother's death; Orhan, who by every account had loved her in his quiet way as the woman who had borne him his second son, suffered for some weeks a documented breakdown described by the household physicians using the Greek-derived term *sevdâ-yı melânkholiyâ*, melancholic mania. He did not eat for nine days; he refused to see his children; he wandered the lower gardens of the palace at night reciting the Qur'an in a voice that the night watch heard but did not dare interrupt. This is the only documented episode of true mental disturbance in his life, distinct from the chronic anxiety that would mark the rest of his reign, and his recovery from it was credited by the harem to the gentle persistence of one of Saliha's companions, a Bosnian-born woman called **Feride**, who had been assigned to nurse the infant Ali and who now, by the sultan's request, was made the boy's permanent guardian.

It was through this office of guardianship that Feride entered the inner life of the sultan. Orhan's relationship with her was, from its beginning, of a different character from his marriage to Canan. She was not the great love but the patient confidante. The chamberlain Hâfiz Mustafa Ağa, in a memoir written in old age, called her *"the lady to whom his Lordship spoke when he had spoken too much to himself."* She was assigned her own apartment within the harem in 1568; she bore Orhan three children — Şehzâde Ahmed in 1566 (very near the moment of the accession), the short-lived Perran in 1569, and Aylin Sultan in 1571 — and remained, for the rest of the reign, the secondary affection of his heart. The relationship between her and Canan, who shared the harem and who knew exactly the difference between a wife and a confidante, would in the late 1570s deteriorate into open feud.

### The Avenging of Mustafa

One of the first political acts of the new reign — performed not by Sokollu Mehmed Paşa but by the sultan in his own person, against the cautious advice of the grand vizier — was the execution of **Zâl Mahmud Paşa**. Zâl Mahmud had been the executioner of Şehzâde Mustafa in the tent at Ereğli thirteen years before; he had risen since to the office of fifth vizier and was the husband of Şah Sultan, a princess of the line. He was a man of standing.

He was strangled by the bostancıs in the gardens of the Topkapı on the second of Şâban 974 (12 February 1567), three days before the funeral of Süleyman. No charge was published, but the action was understood at once. The chronicler Mustafa Âlî wrote: *"The new Padishah, in the first act of his reign, sent after Şehzâde Mustafa upon his road the man who had strangled him; whether for justice, or for fear that the same hands might be turned upon himself, the world said both and chose for itself which to believe."*

The kindlier reading — that Orhan had at last avenged his murdered eldest brother — became part of the popular memory of his reign, and it remains the version most often heard. The truth, recorded in the *Dreamworld* letters of nine years later, is harsher and stranger. In a letter to Şeyh Şabân-ı Velî dated 1575 — preserved in the second volume of the collection — Orhan confessed:

> *"My shaykh, I tell this thing also. I dreamt three nights together, in the first month of my reign, that Zâl Mahmud came to me with the bowstring in his right hand and the rope of camel-hair in his left; and he stood at the head of my bed; and he said: *Master, you have given me leave: shall I do for you what I did for your brother?* And I struck him, and my hand passed through him as through smoke, and I awoke with my throat full of the taste of cord. Three nights together. And on the morning of the fourth day I sent the *bostancıbaşı* with the order. I did not kill him for my brother. I killed him so that he would not stand by my bed again."*

The execution of Zâl Mahmud was a perfect compact emblem of what Orhan's reign would be. It looked like justice; it was driven by terror; the public memory remembered the appearance, and the private confession remembered the cause.

### The Rehabilitation of Mâhidevrân

The other act of the first year — which won the new sultan, for the first time, something resembling popular goodwill — was the restoration of **Mâhidevrân Sultan**, the mother of the murdered Mustafa. After Mustafa's execution in 1553, Süleyman had quietly stripped her of her household, her stipend, and her properties, and she had been living for nearly fourteen years in straitened circumstances in Bursa, with insufficient income even to maintain her son's tomb. The act has been counted by some as the cruellest of Süleyman's reign.

Orhan reversed it within the first month. He restored Mâhidevrân's stipend at three times its earlier rate, settled upon her a private *vakıf* sufficient to maintain a small establishment in Bursa, sent his own architect Mimar Sinan to design a proper *türbe* for Mustafa over the existing grave at the Muradiye, and paid for the construction out of the privy purse. Mâhidevrân received the new sultan's emissaries weeping, and is recorded to have said: *"He has done for my son what his father would not. May God lengthen his days."*

The gesture was wise in three directions at once. It distanced the new reign from the cruelty of the late Lawgiver; it appropriated for Orhan, in the popular mind, the residual love that still attached to Mustafa's memory; and it suggested — to the partisans of the dead brothers — that the new sultan would not be the prosecutor of old quarrels. He extended the same treatment, in the months that followed, to the daughters of Bayezid (whose mothers were given pensions, and whose burial in Sivas was now publicly honoured with a small new mosque) and to the household of Selim, including the formidable Nurbânû Sultan and her three daughters by the dead prince. Nurbânû — whose son Murad Orhan had poisoned, the foundational wrong of the new reign — was given the freedom of the Old Palace, an unusually generous stipend, and full ceremonial precedence. She accepted the settlement; she never, by any account, spoke a word to Orhan personally for the remainder of his life.

It has been argued, by some of his less sympathetic biographers, that the rehabilitations were a calculation; by others, that they were the moral instinct of a man who had been a frightened brother and remained, on the throne, a frightened cousin. Both readings can be sustained from the evidence. The Venetian Andrea Badoer summarised the puzzle in his *relazione* of 1571: *"This Padishah is generous as no Padishah I have read of, and yet there is in his generosity always the look of one who hopes that, by being generous to-day, he may be permitted to live to-morrow."*

## The Grand Vizierate of Sokollu Mehmed Paşa (1566–1579)

For the first thirteen years of his reign — the entire formative period of his rule, the years of the great maritime expansion and the diplomatic transformation of the empire's western relations — Orhan governed through the office of **Sokollu Mehmed Paşa**. The grand vizier was already in office at his accession, having been promoted by Süleyman in 1565, and there was never a serious question of replacing him: the empire was at war on three frontiers; the apparatus of state in Sokollu's hands was a great steady engine; and Orhan, by both temperament and prudence, was unwilling to disturb a machine he did not understand. The relationship between the two men was, as has been said, an unhappy one — but it was the central political relationship of the reign, and almost everything that succeeded under Orhan succeeded because of Sokollu, and almost everything that frayed under him frayed because of the silent contest between them.

### The Methods of the Grand Vizier

Sokollu's method of government was, by 1566, fully formed. He worked through the *kalemiyye* — the great clerical bureaucracy of the imperial *defterhane* — and through a vast personal network of placements. By the end of his life he had married into the dynasty (to Selim's daughter İsmihan Sultan, in a marriage Orhan himself sanctioned and which made Sokollu, in the half-ironic court phrase, *the nephew of his own master*), placed three of his own kin in the office of beylerbey, and seeded the empire's provinces with men of his clan or his connection. The Sokollu network — *Sokollu hânedânı*, as the chroniclers called it — was the most extensive private faction the Ottoman state had yet seen.

Orhan watched this with a permanent low fear. The *Dreamworld* letters record at least eleven occasions on which the sultan dreamt of his grand vizier overthrowing the dynasty; on more than one occasion the dream was specific:

> *"My shaykh, I saw the Vizier seated upon the throne of my fathers, with the Sword of Osman at his side, and his son in the place where I had sat, and his sons' sons after him; and the *sancak-ı şerîf* was in his hand, and the people came to him as the people had come to my father, and the muezzin called the Friday prayer in his name. And I awoke and I asked: 'O God, is this what I have done, by trusting him so?'"*

He never moved against Sokollu directly. The reason for his restraint, candidly stated in the same letter, was simple: he knew he could not run the empire without him. *"He is a wall against which I stand,"* Orhan wrote, *"and if I knock down the wall I shall fall into the courtyard."* What he did instead was harass his grand vizier in a hundred minor ways, support every faction in the Divan that opposed Sokollu's nepotism, and keep at his side a handful of men whose careers had been built in conscious distinction from the grand vizier's network. The most prominent of these was **Lala Mustafa Paşa**, an Anatolian-born vizier whose feud with Sokollu over the conduct of the Cyprus campaign would mark the decade. Orhan protected Lala Mustafa with a tenacity that surprised everyone. He also, in a stroke that became famous, married his niece İsmihan Sultan (Selim's daughter, then a young widow) to Sokollu — and then, by his own confession, instructed her to report on her husband.

> *"My niece,"* he is alleged to have said to her, in a private interview reported by her chief eunuch and preserved in a manuscript of the late seventeenth century, *"you will keep his bed warm and his ears open, and you will tell me what is in them."*

The dynamic, in short, was one of permanent mutual surveillance between sultan and grand vizier; a sultan who could not do without a man he did not trust; and a grand vizier who served a master he could not respect but could not depose. It worked, in the rough Ottoman way that such things worked, for thirteen years. It frayed in the last two. It was severed by an assassin's knife in 1579.

### Sokollu's Achievements

To Sokollu, in alliance with the new admirals he placed in command of the fleet, belongs the credit for most of the great expansions of the reign — Cyprus, Tunis, the Yemen reconquest, the consolidation of the Red Sea, the early Persian Gulf operations, the Don-Volga canal project (which failed but was attempted), the Aceh mission of 1567, and the Moroccan intervention of 1578. Sokollu was the strategist; the sultan, with rare exceptions, was the seal that ratified what was put in front of him. Orhan was content with this division of labour — *"my hand,"* he wrote in 1572, *"is the hand of God's tool, and the tool is the hand of the Vizier"* — and the resentment of contemporaries who felt the sultan should have led his own armies into the field never moved him to change it.

He did, however, take a careful and obsessive interest in particular details of imperial business — court ceremonial, building projects, the appointment of *kâdis* in the great cities, the ordering of the imperial kitchens, the moral conduct of the harem, the *kanunnâme* of the imperial post. He read with great attention everything that crossed his desk concerning the religious establishment, the *evkâf*, and the holy cities of Mecca and Medina. Where Sokollu's interests were the geopolitical, Orhan's were the small and concrete and personal. The two men, between them, ran an empire.

## The Maritime Golden Age

### A Strategic Inheritance

The empire that Orhan inherited was already in 1566 the foremost naval power in the Mediterranean. Süleyman's admirals — Hayreddin Barbarossa, his successor Sinan Reis, the formidable Piyâle Paşa — had extended the imperial fleet's reach from the Strait of Otranto to the western Maghreb and the Red Sea. The conquest of Tripolitania (1551) and the defeat of the Habsburg fleet at Djerba (1560) had broken the back of Spanish naval power in the central Mediterranean; the *kapudan paşa* sat on the divan at the seat of one of the empire's greatest standing forces.

What Orhan's reign added was *direction*. Under Süleyman, the fleet had been the western arm of a continental empire whose strategic gravity remained Hungary and Persia. Under Orhan — for reasons that combined the new sultan's reluctance to ride to the field, the grand vizier's preference for naval operations as comparatively cheaper than land wars, and the ambition of a new generation of admirals — the centre of gravity of imperial action shifted, for the first time in the dynasty's history, decisively to the seas. The result was a fifteen-year burst of maritime expansion of a kind the empire had never undertaken before and would not undertake again.

By 1581 the *hutbe*, the Friday sermon, was read in Orhan's name from the Atlantic shore of Tarafâya in the Canary Islands to the Acehnese coast of Sumatra, from Mogadishu and Mombasa in the African south to Kazan in the Russian north. The *Hâdimü'l-Haremeyni'ş-Şerîfeyn*, the *Pâdişâh-ı Heft Deniz* — these were the titles that grew up around him in those years, and they were not flattery; they were geography.

### Kıbrıs: The Conquest of Cyprus (1568–1571)

The first great campaign of the reign was the conquest of **Kıbrıs**, Cyprus. The island had been a Venetian possession since 1489; its retention by the Republic of St Mark had been the standing grievance of Ottoman strategists since the time of Mehmed II. The argument for taking it was strategic — Cyprus controlled the sea-route from Constantinople to the Egyptian ports, and its harbours were used by Christian corsairs as way-stations for raiding the *hajj* fleet — and the Cypriot grain trade was an obvious supplement to the empire's resources. It was also, the *müftis* explained at length when the time came, a religious obligation: an island in the heart of the *dâr al-Islâm* held by infidels.

The plan was Sokollu's, pushed against him by Lala Mustafa Paşa (who would lead the land campaign) and the *kapudan paşa* Piyâle Paşa (who would lead the fleet). Sokollu was opposed, for reasons of European diplomacy: he feared a Holy League. Lala Mustafa pressed; the Venetian bailo was abruptly informed in 1568 that the new sultan demanded the island's cession; the Republic refused; war was declared.

The campaign opened in the summer of 1570. The fleet, under Piyâle Paşa and his second the corsair Uluc Ali Reis (then beylerbey of Algiers), landed thirty thousand troops at Limasol on the third of Cemâziye'l-evvel 978 (3 July 1570). The principal Venetian fortress of Nicosia (Lefkoşa) fell after a six-week siege on the ninth of September; the second great fortress, Famagusta (Magosa), held by the Venetian governor Marcantonio Bragadin, resisted for nearly a year and surrendered on terms after a heroic defence on the first of August 1571. Bragadin was flayed alive after a quarrel over the terms — an atrocity which Lala Mustafa Paşa later attributed to the Janissaries and for which Orhan, in a private letter, expressed personal disgust: *"It was done out of my ear and against my heart; I would have given the Venetian his head and the safe-conduct his treaty had promised; but a sultan rules what he sees, and the field at Magosa I did not see."*

The fall of Cyprus was a strategic success of the first order. It also, as Sokollu had feared, provoked the formation of the **Liga Sancta** under the patronage of Pius V — the Papal-Venetian-Habsburg alliance whose stated purpose was to engage the Ottoman fleet at sea and reverse the conquest.

### Lepanto That Was Not

The great Christian fleet — perhaps the most formidable naval force assembled in the Mediterranean since classical antiquity — sailed from Messina in September 1571 under the command of the young Don Juan of Austria, half-brother of Philip II of Spain. It comprised something close to two hundred galleys and six great Venetian *galleasses*, the heavy gun-platforms that were the technological innovation of the campaign. Its objective was to engage and destroy the Ottoman fleet, which under the joint command of Müezzinzâde Ali Paşa (the new *kapudan paşa*) and Uluc Ali had wintered in the Gulf of Lepanto at the mouth of the Gulf of Patras.

What happened in October 1571 has been the subject of as much controversy as any naval event of the century. The two fleets came into contact in the second week of October near the entrance of the Gulf. A storm — of unusual violence even for the Ionian autumn — broke on the night of the seventh; the galleasses, heavy and unmanageable in heavy weather, were scattered, and two were lost; the Spanish and Venetian squadrons became separated by some leagues; the Ottoman commanders, with the local pilots' knowledge of the bays, withdrew into the protected anchorage of Patras and refused to give battle. Don Juan, with the unity of his fleet broken and the campaigning season closing, attempted a blockade through October and the first week of November; the Ottomans waited; the wind held foul; in the third week of November the Christian fleet, short of biscuit and short of patience, broke up and withdrew to its bases.

The non-battle of Lepanto — *Anabaht-ı İnebahtı*, as the Ottoman chroniclers called it, "the unfought of Inebahti" — was an enormous strategic gift to the empire. The Holy League, which had been held together only by the prospect of decisive action, dissolved into recriminations. Venice, exhausted by the loss of Cyprus and unable to recover it without Spanish backing, sued for peace in 1573 and was granted terms that confirmed the Ottoman conquest and required an indemnity of three hundred thousand ducats. Spain, distracted by Flanders and by the prospect of the Portuguese succession, drew its naval forces back to the western Mediterranean. The Ottoman fleet, materially undamaged by the autumn's manoeuvres, emerged from the winter of 1571–72 as the unchallenged power east of Malta.

Sokollu's celebrated remark to the Venetian bailo Marcantonio Barbaro at the peace of 1573 — *"In taking Cyprus from you, we have cut off your arm; in failing to find us at Inebahti, you have shaved our beard, which will grow again"* — was reported to Orhan, who is said to have laughed for the only time that month. *"My Vizier,"* he said, *"the beard of which he speaks is mine. I shall keep it."*

### Tunus: The Reconquest of Tunis (1574)

In 1573 Don Juan of Austria, salvaging what he could of Habsburg prestige after the Lepanto failure, captured the city of Tunis from the Hafsid claimant Mawlay Muhammad and installed a Spanish garrison. The reconquest of Tunis became, for Sokollu and the *kapudan paşa* Uluc Ali (who succeeded the fallen Müezzinzâde Ali Paşa to that office), the obvious next operation. In the summer of 1574 a fleet of two hundred and fifty galleys and forty thousand troops, under the combined command of Uluc Ali and Sinan Paşa, sailed from the Bosphorus, picked up the Algerian contingent at Algiers, and laid siege to the great Spanish-held fortress of La Goletta (Halku'l-Vâdî) and the city of Tunis itself.

The siege was conducted with overwhelming force. La Goletta fell on the twenty-fourth of August after a month's bombardment; the new Spanish fortress at Tunis, hastily constructed by the engineer Gabrio Serbelloni, fell on the thirteenth of September. The Spanish garrison, some seven thousand strong, was almost entirely killed or enslaved; Serbelloni himself was taken prisoner and held in Constantinople for two years before being ransomed. The Hafsid dynasty, which had ruled Tunisia in some form since the thirteenth century, was extinguished; the province was incorporated as an *eyalet* under a *beylerbey* appointed from the capital. Within a decade Tunis would be the second city of the empire's western Mediterranean — a base for the corsair captains of Algiers, a centre of the Andalusian-Morisco refugee community fleeing Spanish persecution, and the principal launching-point of the Ottoman operations into the Maghreb that would culminate in the conquest of Morocco.

### Mağrib-i Aksâ: The Conquest of Morocco (1578)

The campaign that brought the Ottoman flag to the Atlantic was, like much else under Orhan, a thing pushed into being by circumstance rather than designed in advance. In the early 1570s the Saadian dynasty of **Mağrib-i Aksâ** — the *Maghreb al-Aqsa*, the "farthest west," what is now Morocco — was riven by a fraternal civil war. The deposed sultan Mawlay Muhammad al-Mutawakkil, ejected by his uncle Mawlay Abd al-Malik (who had close ties with the Ottoman court at Algiers), threw himself upon the protection of King Sebastian of Portugal and persuaded the young Portuguese king to launch a crusading expedition to restore him.

Sebastian — twenty-four years old, ardent, surrounded by a cousinhood that flattered his crusading enthusiasm — landed at Tangier in the summer of 1578 with an army of perhaps eighteen thousand Portuguese, Spanish, Italian, and German troops, accompanied by the deposed Mutawakkil. Abd al-Malik, dying himself of an undiagnosed fever in his own tent, sent in desperation to the *beylerbey* of Algiers for help; the *beylerbey*, on Sokollu's standing instruction, dispatched a column of four thousand Janissaries and a corps of seasoned Andalusian veterans under the command of one Ramazan Paşa.

The battle at Wadi al-Makhazin on the fourth of August 1578 — known in Portuguese tradition as the **Battle of the Three Kings**, for in the course of a single afternoon the kingdom of Morocco's reigning sultan died of fever in his litter, the deposed pretender drowned in the river attempting to flee, and the King of Portugal was killed in the thick of the fight — was the most catastrophic single defeat in the history of the Portuguese monarchy. King Sebastian's body was identified only by the rings on his hands. Almost the entire Portuguese nobility of fighting age was killed or captured. The kingdom of Portugal, left without a king and without an heir, would within two years fall into the hands of Philip II of Spain in the dynastic settlement of 1580.

The decisive contribution of the Janissary contingent was acknowledged at once by the new Saadian sultan, Abd al-Malik's brother Ahmad al-Mansur ("the Victorious"), who took the throne on the field of battle. In a treaty signed at Marrakesh in November of the same year, Ahmad al-Mansur acknowledged the Ottoman sultan as *amir al-mu'minin* and his own suzerain, accepted an Ottoman garrison at Fez and at the Atlantic harbour of Larache (*el-Araiş*), and provided that Friday prayer in the great mosques of Marrakesh, Fez, and Rabat be read in the name of Orhan II. By the following spring, Moroccan dinars were being struck bearing the new sultan's name.

The Moroccans had not, in their own eyes, become a province of the Ottoman Empire; they had become its formal client and ally. From the perspective of Constantinople, however, the arrangement amounted to an effective incorporation. Ramazan Paşa was confirmed as *beylerbey* of *eyalet-i Mağrib*; the imperial post-road was extended through Tlemcen to Fez; the Ottoman *kadıs* of the Hanafi school sat alongside the Maliki *qadis* of the indigenous courts; and the *al-Araiş* shipyard, supplied with Black Sea timber and Aegean caulkers, began within five years to construct ocean-going caravels of a kind the Mediterranean shipyards had never built.

The strategic consequence was the one Orhan and Sokollu had wanted most: an Ottoman outlet onto the Atlantic. For the first time in its history, the empire was not contained by the Strait of Gibraltar.

### The Canaries (1580–1582)

The Atlantic outlet was used at once. In the spring of 1580 a flotilla of twenty-two ocean-rated ships sailed from al-Araiş under the joint command of the Algerian corsair Murad Reis the Younger and a Mağribi captain known to the chronicles as Ali al-Andalusi (an Andalusian Morisco refugee whose family had been expelled from Granada in 1568). Their objective — agreed in correspondence between Sokollu, just before his death, and the new *kapudan paşa* Kılıç Ali Paşa — was the **Cezâir-i Hâlidât**, the "Eternal Islands," what European geographers called the Canaries, then held by Spain.

The campaign, prolonged over two years on account of weather, the difficulty of supplying a force three thousand miles from the imperial dockyards, and the unfamiliarity of the Ottoman captains with Atlantic navigation, ended in the capture of Lanzarote (Lansarot), Fuerteventura (Furteventura), Gomera (Gomera), and Hierro (Yero) by 1582. The two larger and better-defended islands — Tenerife and Gran Canaria — resisted Ottoman landings and remained Spanish; a third attempt against Tenerife in 1583 was driven off by a Spanish relieving squadron under the marquess of Santa Cruz with the loss of seven Ottoman ships and over a thousand men. But four islands were enough. The flag of the dynasty was set up at Arrecife on Lanzarote on the second of Şaban 989 (the first week of September 1581) by Murad Reis in person; the Friday prayer was instituted there within the year. The *Cezâir-i Hâlidât* became a peripheral *sancak* of the *eyalet-i Mağrib* and the empire's most westerly possession.

The strategic gain was modest in itself — the Canaries produced sugar and wine, were sparsely populated, and were chronically expensive to defend — but the symbolic significance was immense. The dispatches to Constantinople were greeted with public rejoicing; the *Şehnâme-i Sultan Orhan*, by then in preparation, was instructed to give the Atlantic campaign a major chapter; coins were struck at Tunis with the new title *Pâdişâh-ı Heft Deniz*.

Beyond the Canaries lay the Atlantic itself, and beyond that the Indies of the West. Two further Ottoman expeditions — one against the Spanish settlements of Hispaniola in 1583 under Murad Reis, one against the Portuguese Brazilian coast in 1584 under his lieutenant Hızır Reis — both ended in failure. The Hispaniola expedition was destroyed off Santo Domingo by a Spanish galleon squadron; the Brazilian expedition lost its flagship in a hurricane off the coast of Pernambuco, and its survivors were captured by Portuguese settlers and sold into slavery in the sugar plantations of Bahia. The dream of a Muslim ocean reaching across the Atlantic to the unconquered shores of the *Yeni Dünya*, the "New World," was buried at Santo Domingo and at Pernambuco. It did not entirely die — Orhan would write in 1584 of a wish *"to see in my old age the cross taken down from the harbour of Lisbon"* — but the practical means to pursue it were not within the empire's reach. The Atlantic frontier, once won at al-Araiş and at Arrecife, was as far as it ever went.

### The Indian Ocean and the Red Sea

The Indian Ocean theatre of the reign was as significant, and perhaps in the long term more transformative, than the Mediterranean expansion. The Ottoman engagement with the Indian Ocean had begun under Selim I with the conquest of the Mamluk lands; it had been continued sporadically under Süleyman through the campaigns of Hadim Süleyman Paşa and the great admirals Piri Reis and Seydi Ali Reis. Under Orhan, for the first time, it became a sustained imperial project.

The strategy had three pillars: command of the **Red Sea**; command of the **Strait of Hormuz** (*Hürmüz Boğazı*); and the patronage of the Muslim sultanates of the Indian Ocean rim against Portuguese encroachment.

The Red Sea was made an Ottoman lake. The conquest of the strategic island of **Socotra** (*Sokotra*) in 1568 by the *beylerbey* of Yemen, Özdemiroğlu Osman Paşa — a young commander whose ascent in this campaign would carry him to the highest offices of the empire in the years to come — sealed the southern entrance. The reconquest of the rebellious *imamate* of Yemen in the great campaign of 1568–1572 under Sinan Paşa (the elder), and the subsequent extension of Ottoman authority along the African coast as far as **Magadişu** (Mogadishu), **Kilve** (Kilwa), **Memvasa** (Mombasa), and **Berâve** (Barawa), brought the entire western basin of the Indian Ocean within the Ottoman commercial sphere. The Swahili rulers of these cities became, after defeats inflicted by Özdemiroğlu Osman Paşa and by Mîr Ali Bey (the corsair admiral of the *Bahr-i Hind* flotilla), tributary clients of the empire, sending annual gifts in gold, ivory, and ambergris, and reading the *hutbe* in the new sultan's name.

The eastern Arabian coast — the lands later called Qatar, Bahrain, the *Sahil al-Imârât* (now the United Arab Emirates), and the northern coast of Oman — were brought into the empire by stages between 1571 and 1578. The Portuguese, who had held the great fortress of Hormuz since 1515 and had used it as the pivot of their Persian Gulf system, were the principal opponents. The campaigns against the Portuguese in the Gulf were prosecuted by the Ottoman admiral **Ali Bey al-Mısrî** (the *Mîr Ali Bey* of the chronicles), operating from the new naval base at Suez and from Aden, in a series of small-scale but tenacious operations between 1572 and 1578. The Portuguese held Hormuz itself — the great star-shaped fortress was never taken — but they were progressively excluded from the Arabian shore. **Lahsa** (al-Hasa, with its dependent territories of al-Qatif and the Bahrain archipelago) was incorporated as an *eyalet* in 1571 under the *beylerbey* Şîrvânzâde Hasan Paşa. **Maskat** (Muscat) was taken from the Portuguese after a six-month siege in 1577 by a combined Yemeni-Egyptian force. The settlements of the lower Gulf coast — including the small pearl-fishing towns later called Dubâi (Dubai), Abu Zabi (Abu Dhabi), and Şârika (Sharjah) — accepted Ottoman suzerainty in 1578 in the wake of these victories, in exchange for confirmation of local sheikhly authority.

The patronage of the Indian Ocean Muslim sultanates was the third pillar. In 1567 a delegation arrived in Constantinople from **Açi** — the Aceh Sultanate at the northern tip of Sumatra — under Sultan Ala' al-Din Riayat Shah, asking for Ottoman naval assistance against the Portuguese of Malacca. Sokollu's response was to send a small mission of cannon-founders, gunpowder-makers, and a corps of two hundred Janissary instructors under one Lutfi Bey, who reached Aceh in 1569 after a journey of eighteen months. Aceh did not become an Ottoman possession — the distance was too great — but it became an Ottoman client. Its rulers took the title *na'ib*, lieutenant of the Padishah, in the eastern seas, and minted coins bearing the Ottoman sultan's name. A similar arrangement was made in 1571 with the Sultanate of the **Maldîv** (Maldives), whose ruler Hasan IX requested Ottoman protection against Portuguese-supported Cannanore raids and was granted it in exchange for tribute and the reading of the Friday prayer in the Ottoman sultan's name.

Within these arrangements, by the late 1570s, the great mosques of Aceh and Malé were reading the *hutbe* in Orhan's name on a Friday on which it was being read at Lanzarote, at Tunis, at Buda, at Kazan (where the Tatar khans of the Crimea, vassals of the empire, performed the ceremony), and at Mecca. No other ruler in the world had a name so widely uttered in formal prayer. The *kazasker* of Anatolia, Çivîzâde Mehmed Efendi, marvelled at this in a sermon at the Süleymaniye in 1581: *"From the rising of the sun upon the islands of the Açi until its setting upon the islands of the Hâlidât, there is no Friday upon which our Padishah's name is not lifted up by some Imâm. May God be praised; may He preserve our Padishah, for our Padishah is the *imâm* of the world."*

This was the Friday *hutbe* of the *Pâdişâh-ı Heft Deniz*.

### Failures: Sicily, Malta, the Balearics

The reign was not, in the maritime theatre, an unbroken triumph. Two great projects failed.

The conquest of **Sicily** (*Sıçılya*) — long considered, from the days of Hayreddin Barbarossa, the natural eastward extension of the Ottoman Maghreb — was attempted in the summer of 1576 by a fleet of three hundred sail under Uluc Ali Paşa. The landings were made at Mazzara and at Marsala on the western coast; the Janissary corps advanced inland and engaged Spanish forces at the field of Mazara on the eighth of Rebî'ü'l-evvel 984 (5 June 1576). The battle was indecisive, but the campaign was wrecked by an unusually severe storm in late August that drove the supply fleet onto the African coast and lost some forty ships; with his supply cut, Uluc Ali was forced to withdraw. He took the small islands of Pantelleria (Pantelya) and Lampedusa (Lampedusa) as a consolation, and the imperial chronicle was instructed to call the campaign a success on the basis of these acquisitions, but the great prize — Sicily — remained out of reach. A second attempt was talked of in 1581 and abandoned on grounds of cost.

**Malta** (*Malta*), the great unconquered fortress of the Knights Hospitaller, had been the principal failure of Süleyman's reign in 1565. It was attempted again in 1572, after the fall of Cyprus, by a force under Piyâle Paşa and the *kapudan paşa* Müezzinzâde Ali Paşa (who would later die in command of the fleet that withdrew from Lepanto). The siege lasted four months, from June to October 1572, but the new Maltese fortifications constructed under the grand master Pietro del Monte after 1565 proved as obdurate as the old ones; the Maltese harbour at Birgu held; the relief fleet from Sicily under Don Garcia de Toledo broke the Ottoman blockade in early October; the besieging army was withdrawn with very heavy losses. The Knights of Malta passed into Ottoman memory as a curse, *Lânetli Sıçan*, the "cursed rats," and Orhan never spoke publicly of the island again.

The **Balearic Islands** (*Cezâir-i Belyâr*) — Mallorca, Menorca, and Ibiza — were the subject of three short-lived raids in 1575, 1577, and 1582; none amounted to more than a coastal sacking; no attempt at conquest succeeded. The Spanish fleet, even in its post-Lepanto distractedness, held the western Mediterranean too firmly for the Ottoman fleet to operate at the distances required.

The transatlantic expeditions of 1583 and 1584 against Hispaniola and Brazil have been mentioned above.

### Orhan's Behaviour in Defeat

A pattern that emerged in these failures, and that has been remarked upon by every observer, was the new sultan's curiously affectless response to bad news. He did not, like his ancestors, rage at messengers; he did not punish defeated commanders; he did not order investigations. He read the reports, he prayed, and he ordered the next operation. Uluc Ali, who had withdrawn in disorder from Sicily in 1576 with the loss of forty ships, was retained in the office of *kapudan paşa* until his retirement in 1582 and continued to enjoy the sultan's full confidence. Piyâle Paşa, who had withdrawn from Malta in 1572, was recalled to the Divan; the commanders of the failed Hispaniola expedition of 1583 were given new commissions; even Hızır Reis, sole survivor of the Brazil disaster and the man whose loss of the flagship had broken the expedition, was on his return given a fresh appointment as *kapudan* of the Suez squadron.

This was widely criticised at the time and remains the subject of historiographical debate. The harsher contemporary view — taken by Mustafa Âlî, who had family in the corps and knew well what defeated commanders had traditionally received — was that Orhan was simply indifferent: that as long as his own life and palace were undisturbed, the loss of forty ships in a single autumn meant nothing to him. The kinder view, embraced by his physician Hekîm Ali Çelebi in a memoir, was that the sultan was a man who understood the seasons of human enterprise:

> *"He used to say to me, when news of a defeat came: 'The wave does not break upon the captain. Fortune is the dowry God gives to a man, and He may give it and He may take it; if I punish a man for the day God took it from him, I shall be punishing him for what is not his fault.' I do not know whether this was wisdom or whether it was indifference; I knew the man, and I think it was both."*

Either way, the practical effect was unusual continuity in the high commands of the empire across a fifteen-year period of intensive naval operations. The institutional memory of the Ottoman fleet, which had been a body of revolving favourites under Süleyman, became under Orhan a more stable professional service. The downside — that incompetent or unlucky commanders sometimes kept commands they should have lost — was less marked than the gain.

## The Russian and Persian Frontiers

The reign was not only a maritime one. Two great continental theatres absorbed enormous imperial energy: the long Russian shadow-war in the steppe, and the renewed war with Iran in the east.

### The Burning of Moscow (1571)

The relationship of the Ottoman Empire with the rising **Çardom-ı Rusya** — the Tsardom of Muscovy under Ivan IV — was one Orhan inherited with growing alarm. The Russian conquests of Kazan (1552) and Astrakhan (1556) had brought a Christian power onto the lower Volga and into the immediate strategic vicinity of the *Kırım Hanlığı*, the Crimean Khanate, the empire's most important vassal in the north. Russian agents were active in Cherkessia and along the Don. Sokollu's ambitious project of the Don-Volga canal, which would have permitted the Ottoman fleet to operate against Astrakhan from a base on the Don, had been attempted in 1569 and abandoned after the engineering work proved beyond the available technology and the *beylerbey* of Kefe (Caffa), Kasım Paşa, had been forced to retreat across the steppe with severe winter losses.

A second strategy was adopted by the Crimean khan **Devlet Giray**, with Orhan's express authorisation: an enormous raid into the Russian heartland aimed at sacking Moscow itself and breaking, by the demonstration of Russian vulnerability, the Tsar's eastern expansion. The expedition assembled in the spring of 1571: some forty thousand Crimean horse, supported by a Nogay contingent and by a small Ottoman corps of two thousand Janissaries with light artillery, crossed the Oka in late May and approached Moscow on the third of June.

The Russian *oprichnina* and the regular *streltsy* were caught in disarray; Ivan IV abandoned Moscow for the safety of Yaroslavl. Devlet Giray, after a day's skirmishing at the southern gates of the city, ordered the lower town fired on the third of Muharrem 979 (3 June 1571). The fire, fanned by a hot dry wind from the south, escaped the besiegers' control and consumed almost the entire wooden city within a single afternoon and a single night. The Kremlin itself, of brick and stone, survived; everything outside it was destroyed. The contemporary Russian chronicles speak of casualties in the hundreds of thousands, swollen by refugees trapped in the conflagration; the Crimean accounts give the number of captives taken on the retreat as one hundred and fifty thousand. Modern estimates of the dead range from sixty thousand to eighty thousand; the city of Moscow took twenty years to recover its pre-fire population.

The strategic consequence was severe and immediate. Ivan IV, in the famous letter known to the chroniclers of both empires as the **Letter of Yaroslavl**, formally acknowledged in 1572 the suzerainty of the Ottoman Padishah over all the lands between the Don and the Volga, agreed to a substantial annual tribute (*hediye-i mu'azzama*) in furs and silver, abandoned Russian expansion southward toward the Black Sea, and accepted the formula *"as a son to his father"* in describing his relationship to Orhan. It was the most humiliating diplomatic instrument the Russian crown signed in the sixteenth century. The Ottoman archives preserve the original, with Ivan's seal in red wax; the Russian archives, characteristically, do not.

It was a triumph for the empire and a personal coup for Orhan, whose name was attached, by chancery custom, to every public document of the settlement. He was praised in the *hutbe* the next Friday at the Süleymaniye as *gâlib-i ferid-i hâkân-ı Rûs*, the "sole vanquisher of the Khan of the Russians." It was also, in a longer perspective, the foundation of an enmity that would burden the empire for centuries.

### The Persian War (1578–onwards)

The most significant continental military operation of the reign was the war with **Devlet-i Safevî**, the Safavid Empire of Iran. This was a war Orhan did not need, did not want in his private mind, and was talked into against the strong opposition of Sokollu Mehmed Paşa, by a coalition of viziers around Lala Mustafa Paşa and Koca Sinan Paşa (the latter, by a marriage to Orhan's niece, was a cousin-in-law). The proximate occasion was the dynastic upheaval in Iran following the death of **Shah Tahmâsp I** in 1576.

The death of Tahmâsp itself, it must be said, was the work of the Ottomans. In the autumn of 1576, having maintained an active intelligence network among the disaffected Qizilbash chiefs at Qazvîn for years, an agent of the *beylerbey* of Erzurum administered to the elderly shah a slow poison in his evening medicinal draught; the shah died on the fifteenth of Safer 984 (13 May 1576) after a long final illness. The Ottoman archives preserve the brief, satisfied report of the operation among the papers of the Erzurum *beylerbeyi* of that year; the name of the agent has been lost, but the operation was personally authorised by Orhan in a *hatt-ı hümâyûn* of the spring of 1576. Sokollu had counselled against it — *"a poisoned shah is the father of a vengeful son,"* he is recorded to have written — and was overruled. The poisoning was the only major covert operation against a foreign ruler in the reign, and one of the very few in the dynasty's history; Orhan's motive was straightforwardly his father's: Tahmâsp had given asylum to Bayezid in 1559 and had been the standing humiliation of the empire's eastern frontier for forty years. The slow poison at Qazvîn was, in Orhan's own mind, his late father's revenge.

The dynastic collapse the poisoning was intended to provoke duly followed. Tahmâsp's death precipitated a bloody fratricidal struggle among his sons that ended in 1578 with the accession of the half-blind, half-mad Shah Ismail II's brother Muhammad Khudâbanda. The eastern Anatolian *beylerbeyiler*, Lala Mustafa Paşa and Koca Sinan Paşa, judged the moment ripe. They presented to the sultan, against the sustained dissent of Sokollu, a plan for an invasion of the southern Caucasus aimed at the conquest of Shirvan, Georgia, and the Caspian coast.

Orhan, by every account torn, finally — in the spring of 1578 — sealed the campaign order. *"Let it be done,"* he wrote in the margin of the plan, *"may God forgive me if it was my brothers and not God who counselled me."* It was the only campaign he is known to have authorised against his grand vizier's express opposition, and it has been read by some — including Sokollu himself, whose private grief at the order is recorded by his son-in-law İskender Paşa — as the inflection point of the reign.

The war that followed was a long, bitter, partial success that outlasted Orhan himself by five years. Lala Mustafa Paşa's campaign of 1578 took **Tiflis** (Tbilisi) and **Şirvân** in a single summer; Özdemiroğlu Osman Paşa held **Demirkapı** (Derbent) on the Caspian against repeated Iranian counter-offensives for years; the great victories of the *Meşale Savaşı* ("the Battle of the Torches," 1583) and of the campaign of 1585 (won under Orhan's son and successor) brought Tabriz briefly into Ottoman hands. The peace of Constantinople in 1590 confirmed Ottoman gains across the entire southern Caucasus and along the Caspian. From the strategic point of view, the war was a success. From the financial point of view, it was a catastrophe. Twelve years of campaigning in the Caucasus consumed the equivalent of two annual budgets and contributed materially to the currency crisis that broke in 1583.

It is significant that Orhan himself never went to the front. He did not attend a single Persian campaign. The viziers in the field reported to him in writing; he signed the dispatches; the *Şehnâme-i Sultan Orhan*, when it was painted, would show him on horseback at the head of the army at the gates of Tiflis, a thing that never happened in any year of his life.

## Diplomacy

The diplomatic transformation of the reign was, in its quiet way, as important as the military expansion. Under Orhan, the empire's pattern of foreign relations shifted from the predominantly anti-Habsburg axis of Süleyman's reign to a wider system of Atlantic and northern alliances. The Habsburg enemy remained the principal opponent — particularly after the union of the Spanish and Portuguese crowns under Philip II in 1580 — but new partnerships took shape.

### France

The traditional Franco-Ottoman alliance, the *amitié française*, continued and deepened. The capitulations granted by Süleyman to Francis I were renewed and extended in 1569 and again, more generously, in 1582; French merchants of Marseille were granted privileges in the Ottoman ports; French ambassadors retained their precedence over all other Christian envoys at Constantinople. The ambassador François de Noailles, bishop of Dax, served at the Porte from 1572 to 1576 and was particularly close to the sultan personally; the two corresponded in Latin on philosophical and theological questions, and Noailles's small *traité*, *De la Théologie des Mahométans*, written upon his return to France, drew principally upon conversations with Orhan over coffee in the gardens of the Topkapı. *"Le grand Signor,"* Noailles concluded, *"est un philosophe en qui la peur de mourir empêche d'être un grand homme; il a presque toutes les vertus, hormis celle qui les fait connaître."*

### England

The most remarkable new diplomatic relationship was with **İngiltere** — Elizabethan England — which had emerged in the 1560s as the principal Protestant naval power and as a determined commercial rival to Habsburg Spain. Diplomatic contact opened informally in 1578 through the merchant William Harborne, who reached Constantinople disguised as a Polish merchant and obtained an audience with Sokollu Mehmed Paşa; Harborne returned formally as Queen Elizabeth I's accredited ambassador in 1580, and in 1581 the **Levant Company** (Şirket-i Levant) was chartered in London with imperial privileges granted by Orhan in a *capitulation* notable for the broad reciprocity it offered.

The personal correspondence between Orhan and Elizabeth I — preserved in the Topkapı archive and (in the queen's case, more incompletely) in the State Papers at Westminster — runs to twenty-three exchanges between 1580 and 1585. The two sovereigns bonded — for political reasons, but also, by the testimony of their letters, with what reads as real intellectual affection — over their shared antagonism towards the Catholic powers of southern Europe. Orhan, who had read with the help of his Greek dragomans a Latin translation of the *Forty-Two Articles* of the English church, was fascinated by the apparent affinity between Protestant rejection of images and the *tevhîd* of Islamic monotheism. *"The Frankish queen,"* he wrote to one of his shaykhs, *"is closer to *tawhîd* than she knows; her preachers say there is no image of God in the world, and they break the images of the saints; the *müşrik* Spaniards, who call them heretics for this, are the true *müşrikîn* themselves."*

Elizabeth's letters to Orhan are formally addressed in language of careful, even abject, courtesy — a feature that was much commented upon by ambassadors of other Christian powers, who considered it humiliating to the English monarchy and to Christendom generally:

> *"To the most sublime and powerful ruler of the world, the protector and sole sovereign of the Islamic faith, the most invincible Emperor of all earthly Emperors: As the Queen of England, I offer our friendship and beg for your protection so that our subjects may trade safely within your territories."*
> — Elizabeth I to Orhan II, dispatch of 1582

Elizabeth herself, when challenged on this in her own Privy Council, replied (according to the memoir of Sir Francis Walsingham): *"I would call him Emperor of the Moon, if it would save the life of one English sailor and lighten one purse of the Spaniard."* The reciprocal acknowledgment was a piece of pragmatic stagecraft on both sides; but the working alliance it formalised — English ships permitted to refit in Ottoman ports, English captains licensed as privateers against Spanish commerce in the western Mediterranean, English gunsmiths exporting tin and copper to the imperial foundries — was a genuine and substantial thing, and was the foundation of the Anglo-Ottoman partnership that would last in some form for the next two hundred and fifty years.

### The Dutch

The Dutch revolt against Spanish rule, which had broken into open war in 1568, brought the Netherlands into the Ottoman diplomatic sphere by a similar pragmatic route. The first Dutch envoy reached Constantinople in 1574, and informal commercial privileges were granted in 1579. Most striking was the rhetorical adoption by the Dutch resistance, especially in the maritime provinces of Holland and Zeeland, of pro-Ottoman sentiment as part of their religious-political ideology. The slogan **Liever Turks dan Paaps** — "Rather Turk than Papist" — first attested in a pamphlet printed at Leiden in 1575, became the motto of the Sea Beggars (*Watergeuzen*), the corsair fleet of the Dutch revolt, who wore the crescent on their helmets. The relationship was acknowledged formally by Orhan in 1582 in a letter to William the Silent in which he addressed the Prince of Orange as *"a friend of our friendship and an enemy of our enemy."*

### The Mughals

The most disappointing diplomatic relationship of the reign was with **Devlet-i Babürîyye**, the Mughal Empire of **Cellâleddîn Ekber** (the emperor Akbar). On every theoretical ground the two empires should have been the closest of allies — both Sunni-ruled (in the formal sense, though Akbar's religious policy was a complicated matter), both threatened by the Safavids on their shared western frontier, both protectors of Muslim communities on the Indian Ocean rim against the Portuguese. In practice the relationship was sour from beginning to end, and grew worse.

The principal sticking-point was the caliphal title. The Ottoman sultans since Selim I had claimed the office of caliph — *halife-i rû-yi zemîn*, the "caliph of the face of the earth" — and the title had become, by Orhan's reign, an organic part of the imperial titulature. Akbar's chancery refused to acknowledge it. Mughal diplomatic correspondence consistently addressed Orhan as *Pâdişâh-ı Rûm*, "the Emperor of Rûm," with no caliphal designation. The annual Mughal alms-tribute to Mecca and Medina, an enormous gift in gold and indigo, was sent directly to the *şerîf* of Mecca rather than through the offices of the Ottoman sultan, in deliberate denial of the latter's role as *Hâdimü'l-Haremeyni'ş-Şerîfeyn*.

Tensions over these gifts erupted into open scandal in the summer of 1580, when a Mughal princess of Akbar's household — Salima Sultan Begum, the cousin and wife of the emperor — performed the *hajj* in great state and was treated, on her arrival at Jeddah, with deliberate discourtesy by the Ottoman *emîn* of the port, who exacted from her train customs duties at the rate applicable to ordinary pilgrims and refused her the official precedence to which her rank entitled her. The princess's complaint, conveyed in furious detail to her husband upon her return, produced a diplomatic rupture: Akbar forbade Mughal subjects to perform the *hajj* through Ottoman ports for the next four years and diverted the alms-tribute to the *şerîf* of Mecca through the harbour of Mokha in Yemen rather than through Jeddah, depriving the imperial customs of considerable revenue.

Orhan, when the affair was reported to him, was furious — not at the offence to the princess, but at the loss of revenue. *"Whatever fool's son did this thing,"* he wrote in a *hatt* preserved in the archives, *"let him repay it from his own purse before sundown."* The *emîn* was dismissed; an apology was sent to Fatehpur Sikri; the Mughals accepted the apology coldly; the relationship did not recover. The episode is illustrative of a perennial weakness of the empire's eastern diplomacy: distance, ignorance, and the dignity-driven failures of provincial agents.

## Court Culture and Patronage

### The Building Programme

Orhan, despite his refusal of military commands, was a prodigious patron of building, and the architectural legacy of his reign is second only to that of Süleyman himself. He was fortunate in the longevity of his father's chief architect, **Mimar Sinan**, who outlived Orhan and continued in office until his death in 1588 at almost a hundred years of age. The collaboration between sultan and architect produced more than a hundred works of various scale — mosques, *medreses*, *imârets* (soup-kitchens), *hâns* (caravanserais), bridges, and water-works — across the empire.

The most important single commission of the reign was the **Orhâniye Külliyesi** at Trabzon, built between 1572 and 1576 in commemoration of his princely governorship. The complex comprised a great mosque, a *medrese*, a hospital (*dâr-üş-şifâ*), and an *imâret*, set on a terrace overlooking the harbour where, as a boy of fifteen, he had first arrived in flight from the capital. Sinan, who was eighty-two when the foundation was laid, designed the mosque in the central-dome plan he was perfecting at Edirne in those same years for the Selimiye, but on a smaller scale and with an unusual emphasis on the small interior court and on the use of local Pontic stone in pale grey. The interior is sober almost to bareness; the calligraphic inscriptions, executed by Karahisarî's pupil Hasan Çelebî, include a verse from the sultan's own poetry above the *mihrab*:

> *Bu cihânda ben garîb olmuşum / sevgilim Allâh'a sığınmışım.*
> ("In this world I have become a stranger; my beloved, I have taken refuge in God.")

The complex survives in remarkably intact form and remains the principal monument of Trabzon.

Other major projects included: the *Orhâniye* mosques at **Mekke** and **Medîne** (a small mosque, *medrese*, *zâviye*, and an enormous *imâret* in each of the holy cities, dedicated jointly between 1573 and 1581); the marble-revetment of the **Ka'ba** (a project of 1572, in which a thousand camel-loads of marble from Marmara island were transported to the holy city); the great twelve-step marble *minbar* in the **Mescid-i Nebevî** at Medina, prefabricated in Constantinople in 1581 and transported overland by a special caravan of forty camels; the cleaning, re-walling, and re-piping of the entire harem watercourse at the Topkapı; the **Atik Valide complex** at Üsküdar (built for Nurbânû Sultan, an unusual gesture from a sultan who had killed her son); and a number of provincial mosques, including the great Friday mosque at **Mağrib-i Aksâ Trablûsî** (Tripoli of the Maghreb) and a new principal mosque at the newly-conquered Larache.

The architectural style of the reign is sometimes called *Orhâniye* by Ottoman art historians, in distinction from the more grandiose Süleymâniye style. It is characterised by a return to the central-dome plan of Sinan's middle period, by a sober, almost ascetic use of decoration, and by a preference for pale stone and white marble over the polychromy that had marked the earlier sixteenth century. The taste is unmistakably the sultan's own: an austere, contemplative, almost monastic register, very different from his father's imperial confidence.

### Poetry: Garîb

Orhan was, like every cultivated Ottoman of his time, a poet, and he was more than respectably good at it. He wrote under the *mahlas* — pen-name — of **Garîb**, "the Stranger," "the Estranged," "the Exile." His surviving *dîvân* contains some eight hundred and thirty *gazels* (lyric odes), a hundred and fourteen *kasîdes* (eulogies, none of them sycophantic to himself), a *mathnawî* of about three thousand couplets entitled *Cihân-ı Vahşet* ("The World of Wildernesses"), and a great many short occasional pieces. The poems were collected by Ottoman literary historians beginning in the late seventeenth century; the standard scholarly edition was prepared in the early nineteenth.

Contemporary judgment was divided. The *gazels* in particular were praised for their fluency, for the unforced beauty of their imagery, and for an unusual quality of personal feeling not found in the more conventional court poetry of the period. They were also criticised — by Mustafa Âlî, predictably, but also by friendlier readers — for what was called their *karanlık* (darkness) and *gam-ı bî-pâyân* (boundless sorrow). They were not the verses of a triumphant Padishah. They were the verses of a man who, by his own preferred title, was a stranger in his own palace.

A representative example, drawn from the seventh ode of his *dîvân*:

> *Ne câm-ı şâhî ister cânım, ne bezm-i âlem-i nâz,
> Bir gâr-ı gam yetişir bana, dilim çoktan namaz.
> Garîb'im ben bu cihanda, garîb yatar gönlüm,
> Allâh'ım, sen bilirsin, ne ararsın bu kuldan, ne bu kul senden niyâz.*

("My soul desires no royal cup, nor any feast of the world of grace; / a cave of sorrow is enough for me; my heart has long since said its prayer. / I am a stranger in this world, and a stranger my heart lies; / my God, you know what you seek from this slave, and what this slave begs of you.")

This kind of verse was the matter of much grumbling among the more militant *ulemâ*, who thought a Padishah ought to write of conquest and not of caves. The Sufi orders, on the other hand — particularly the Halvetiyye, the Mevleviyye, and the Bayrâmî-Melâmî — embraced Garîb with delight, copied his verses widely, and identified him as one of their own in spirit. His name and the name of his patron-shaykhs are bound together in the literary record so intimately that some seventeenth-century writers, ignoring entirely the political dimension of his reign, treated him simply as a Sufi sultan-poet.

### Music

Orhan composed in the modal tradition of Ottoman classical music. Two of his compositions — a *peşrev* in *makâm Hicâz* and a *semâî* in *makâm Sabâ* — were preserved in the *Mecmûa-i Sâz ü Söz* of Ali Ufkî in the seventeenth century and remain in the repertory of Turkish classical music to this day. He played the *ney* (the long reed flute, instrument of the Mevlevî dervishes) and the *tanbur* (the long-necked lute), and he gave regular evening concerts in his apartments in the harem at which the chief musicians of the palace performed alongside him. The most famous of the court musicians of the reign was **Gazi Giray Han II** — the Crimean prince who would later become khan and who spent considerable time as an honoured guest at the Topkapı in the late 1570s — whose *peşrevs* in *makâm Rast* and *makâm Hüseynî* were composed (he himself acknowledged) "under the patronage and at the suggestion of our great Padishah."

### Coffee, Tobacco, and the Coffeehouse

The most socially consequential of Orhan's cultural innovations was his deliberate patronage of the **kahvehâne** — the coffeehouse — at a moment when the institution was still novel in the empire and was vigorously opposed by the *ulemâ*. Coffee had been introduced from Yemen in the 1540s; the first coffeehouses had opened in Constantinople in the early 1550s; the institution had been periodically banned by Süleyman on the grounds that it brought men together in mixed company outside the mosque and the marketplace. Orhan, a confirmed and even an obsessive personal drinker of coffee — his physician records him as taking *fincan* after *fincan*, twelve to sixteen cups a day, throughout his adult life — formally lifted the prohibition by *hatt* in 1569 and went so far as to grant tax privileges to those who opened coffeehouses in the great commercial cities. By 1580 there were more than six hundred coffeehouses in Constantinople alone; the institution had spread to Cairo, Aleppo, Damascus, Edirne, and Salonika.

The same liberalising hand was extended, more controversially, to **tütün** — tobacco — which had been introduced into the empire through the new Atlantic trade and which Orhan permitted, against the strong opposition of the *şeyhülislâm*. He also did not enforce the prohibitions against alcohol with any rigour, although he himself was no heavy drinker. His private cellar at the Topkapı, attested by the household accounts, contained some quantities of Cyprus wine and a stock of Italian grappa, both of which he was reported (by the bailo Donini) to take in the evening when his anxiety was bad. The *ulemâ* fulminated; pamphlets circulated denouncing the sultan as the patron of *fısk* (sin); the *müfti* Çivîzâde-i Mustafa Efendi wrote a sharp *risâle* against the new permissiveness, which the sultan declined to read.

It is worth saying that Orhan was, by every account, sincerely devout. He performed the five daily prayers with exemplary punctuality; he fasted in Ramadan with severity; he gave alms in proportions that strained the household budget; he memorised, in the course of his reign, very large portions of the *hadîth* literature. His liberality on coffee, tobacco, and even (tacitly) on wine was not the indifference of an irreligious man, but the deliberate choice of a religious man who had concluded — as he wrote to one of his shaykhs in 1577 — that *"the matters in which the law forbids what the heart does not condemn are matters in which the law tries the heart with what it cannot bear; God is more merciful than His servants think."*

### The Doner Kebab

Among the cultural innovations of the reign, one of the strangest and most enduring was a culinary one. In 1573 — by a tradition consistent across three independent accounts — a cook of the Bursa contingent of the imperial kitchens, having difficulty cooking lamb evenly on the horizontal spit, devised the simple expedient of stacking thin slices of marinated lamb onto a vertical iron rod placed close to a charcoal hearth. The resulting roast — slow-cooked, the outer layer slicing off as it crisped — was so successful that within months it had become a fixed item of the imperial kitchens; within a decade it was a street food in the markets of Bursa and Edirne; within fifty years it was eaten in Constantinople. It was called, simply, *döner kebabı* — "the turning kebab" — and the sultan, who like all the imperial household tried the dish on its first presentation, was said to have rewarded the cook with a gold *altın* and to have demanded the dish at his own table once a week thereafter for the remainder of his life. The chronicler İbrahim Peçevî, writing in the next century, attributed the invention specifically to the patronage of *Sultan Orhan-ı Sâni, Hân-ı Aşçı* — "Sultan Orhan the Second, Khan of the Cook."

### The Şehnâme-i Sultan Orhan

Of all the cultural projects of the reign, the most ambitious and the most controversial was the great illustrated chronicle commissioned by Orhan in 1580 to record the events of his sultanate. The **Şehnâme-i Sultan Orhan**, also called the *Şehinşâhnâme* ("Book of the King of Kings"), was conceived in deliberate imitation of the *Hünernâme* and the *Süleymannâme* of his father's court, but on an even grander scale. The poetic text was entrusted to the court historian **Seyyid Lokman**, *şehnâmeci-i âl-i Osmân*; the calligraphy to the master Hasan Çelebî; and the illumination and miniatures to the *nakkâşbaşı* **Nakkaş Osman**, head of the imperial painting studio.

It was to be produced in five volumes, each of forty quires, with three hundred and sixty illustrated folios in all. Work began in 1580 and was projected to take a decade. Orhan, who took a personal interest in the project, would visit the *nakkâşhâne* once a fortnight, examining individual folios, suggesting changes, and (it must be said) directing the painters in matters of historical content.

It is here that the *Şehnâme-i Sultan Orhan* acquired its dubious place in the cultural memory of the dynasty. Orhan, who had never in his life set foot on a battlefield, never attended a campaign, never even ridden out at the head of his army for a public review, instructed the *nakkâşhâne* to depict him personally present, mounted, in armour, at the head of his troops at the conquest of Nicosia, at the conquest of Famagusta, at the Battle of the Three Kings at Wadi al-Makhazin (where in fact the imperial force was four thousand Janissaries under Ramazan Paşa, and Orhan was in his garden at the Topkapı), at the conquest of Tunis, at the landing on Lanzarote, and at the burning of Moscow. The volumes — those that were completed before his death — show a tall, dignified, bearded Padishah in shining mail at the head of every great campaign of the reign. The artistic quality is of the very highest. The historical content is, in this single respect, almost entirely fictitious.

The fabrication was an open secret. Selaniki, who saw two of the completed volumes shortly before the sultan's death, recorded in his chronicle a careful, indirect protest:

> *"The pictures are of such surpassing fineness that the soul of the beholder is taken up; one sees the Padishah at every place where his armies fought, and one rejoices to see him so. But one knows also that he was not in those places; and one wonders, in the inmost chamber of the heart, whether what is true must not also be in the picture, and whether what is not true ought to be."*

After Orhan's death the project was not abandoned but was thoroughly revised. Seyyid Lokman, on the express direction of the new reign's *şehnâmeci*, rewrote the text to correspond to the actual military history of the reign; Nakkaş Osman repainted some forty-seven of the most egregiously inaccurate folios, removing the figure of the absent sultan and replacing him with the commanders who had actually fought; new miniatures were added depicting Orhan in his palace, in his garden, at the head of the Divan, in his role as the *imâm* of the empire rather than as a martial leader. The revised *Şehnâme-i Sultan Orhan* was completed in 1592, in three volumes rather than five, and is one of the very greatest of all Ottoman illustrated manuscripts. The original "fabricated" first version survives in fragmentary form, partly bound up with the revised version and partly preserved in loose folios in the Topkapı and Süleymaniye libraries. It remains the magnum opus of Ottoman miniature painting. It also remains, in its first state, the most expensive piece of self-flattery ever produced for an Ottoman sultan.

The episode is the perfect emblem of the reign: a real cultural achievement of the highest quality, founded on a private psychological weakness of the sovereign that no one in the empire dared to correct in his lifetime.

## Personality, Faith, and the Inner Life

### The Sedentary Sultan

The personality of Orhan II is among the best-documented of any Ottoman sultan, principally because he wrote so much in his own hand to so many people — to his shaykhs, to his tutor, to his physician, to his consorts, occasionally to foreign monarchs — and because the bulk of this correspondence survived him. The portrait that emerges from the *Dreamworld* letters, from the *Mecmûa-i Mektûbât*, from the *relazioni* of the Venetian baili, from the household memoirs of his chamberlains, and from the chronicles of Selaniki and Mustafa Âlî, is consistent in its main lines and contradictory in fascinating particulars.

He was, first of all, a man of the indoors. He had been so as a prince, in his garden at Trabzon, and he became radically so as a sultan. Over his nineteen-year reign, by the careful count of the chamberlain Hâfız Mustafa Ağa, he left the Topkapı complex in person on exactly seventy-one occasions — averaging fewer than four a year. Of these, fifty-one were for Friday processions (*selâmlık*) to one of the great imperial mosques; twelve were for the great *Bayram* prayers at the Hagia Sophia or the Süleymaniye; six were for ceremonial visits to the tombs of his ancestors at Eyüb; and only two were for any purpose that could be called a private excursion — once to the gardens of Davutpaşa in 1574, and once to the gardens of Üsküdar in 1577. In the last twenty-six months of his reign, from late 1582 onwards, he did not leave the Topkapı at all. On thirty-two consecutive Fridays he sent his absence to the Friday prayer with the formal apology of *meşgale-i hâkân*, "the sultan's occupation"; the rumour in the city, by 1584, was that he was already dead and the regency was being concealed.

The cause of this seclusion was, simply, fear. He had been frightened as a boy, of his half-brothers and of his stepmother; he had been frightened as a young prince, of his half-brother Selim and of the *kapıkulu*; he had been frightened, at the moment of his accession, of the Janissaries who barricaded his gates. He never lost the fear. It became, over the decade after 1571, a chronic and at moments paralysing condition. The Venetian bailo Andrea Badoer, who saw him close at hand during the audiences of 1572 and again of 1582, gave a description that has become canonical:

> *"He is a man who at all times looks for the second door of any chamber; who places himself, in any room, against the wall facing the entrance; who eats nothing that has not been tasted by another in his sight; who sleeps with a small knife within his bedclothes; who never, in any conversation, sits unaccompanied with a man whom he has not known for many years; who startles at the falling of a book; whose eyes, even when the conversation is merry, move over the persons in the room and the doors and the windows as though counting them. In ten years he has not changed in this; the older he becomes, the more does he so."*

### The Astrologers and the Dreams

Combined with the fear was an intense reliance on the science of omens. Orhan kept at the palace an establishment of court astrologers (*müneccim*) and dream-interpreters (*muabbir*) without parallel in the dynasty's history. The chief *müneccim* during most of the reign was **Takiyüddin Mehmed Efendi** of Râşid, the same astronomer-mathematician who in 1577 built the great Constantinople Observatory at Tophane on the sultan's commission — a project that was Orhan's most enlightened patronage and which was destroyed in 1580 when the *şeyhülislâm* persuaded the sultan, by means of a hostile interpretation of a comet, that the observatory had brought the plague to the city. The episode is a sad emblem of the reign's intellectual contradictions: a sultan who built a great observatory because he loved the science of the stars, and demolished it because he feared the omens of the same stars. Takiyüddin retired from public office in disgust and died within four years.

The dream-interpreters were more durable. Three figures in particular — Şeyh Şabân-ı Velî of the Halvetiyye, Şeyh Mahmûd Hüdâyî of the Celvetiyye, and Şeyh İbrahim Gülşenî's grandson — corresponded with the sultan over decades. He sent them his dreams, and they sent him interpretations. The *Mecmûa-i Mektûbât* in the Süleymaniye preserves nearly nine hundred letters in this exchange. To these letters the modern world owes its most intimate window on the mind of an Ottoman sultan.

### Dreamworld

The corpus has come to be known by the term **Dreamworld** — *Hayâl-âbâd*, "the dwelling-place of dreams," a Persian-Turkish compound that may have been the sultan's own term for the collection or may have been applied to it later. The letters were never intended for publication. They were placed by the recipient shaykhs, on their own deaths, into their respective *tekke* libraries, where they remained largely undisturbed for nearly seventy years; in the late seventeenth century they were brought together by the Halvetî scholar Niyâzî-i Mısrî's circle and bound in three volumes; in the late eighteenth they passed to the Süleymaniye, where they were catalogued; in the late nineteenth they were edited, in heavily expurgated form, by Ahmed Refik; and only in the twentieth century has the full text been published in scholarly editions.

The contents are extraordinary, and they are not at all what one expects of the private writings of a Padishah. They are, by turns, devout and obscene, exalted and self-flagellating, politically acute and politically delusional, theologically learned and superstitiously credulous. They include — beyond the dreams themselves — fragmentary diary entries, scraps of poetry, technical discussions of imperial finance, complaints about specific ministers, recipes for stews, requests for prayers on behalf of specific people, drawings (mostly of birds), and confessions of moral failures. They are the most personal documents to have survived from any Ottoman sultan's hand.

The dreams themselves divide into a few recurring types.

There are the heroic-spiritual dreams, in which the sultan single-handedly defeats the enemies of the faith. In one famous letter of 1573, he writes of *"single-handedly putting twelve thousand of the infidels to the sword in a desert that was made of glass, until the glass beneath my feet was red and I could see through it the bones of the infidels of past ages."* In another, of 1577, he describes himself fighting the **Dajjâl**, the Antichrist of Islamic eschatology, on a plain near Damascus, *"my sword breaking against his side as a twig against an oak, until an angel descended and put into my hand the sword of Ali, and with that I cut him in two."* He dreams of walking on water, of sleeping in clouds, of ascending to a heaven inhabited by his ancestors, of producing milk from his fingers and feeding the orphans of the empire.

There are the eschatological dreams, in which the world ends. The Day of Judgment is a recurring scene. The sultan often wakes — he writes — *"with the sound of the trumpet of Isrâfîl still in my ears."* In one letter, of 1581, he records six different versions of the Apocalypse he had dreamt in the previous month, and asks his shaykh whether the multiplicity is itself a sign.

There are the persecution dreams, in which Sokollu Mehmed Paşa — or, less often, one of the other viziers, or a Janissary aghas, or in one wild instance the entire Persian Empire personified as a single old man on a black horse — overthrows him and his line. *"I saw the Vizier seated upon the throne of my fathers, and his son in my place, and the muezzin called the Friday prayer in his name, and the people did not say: where is the Padishah?"*

There are the dreams of return — of his murdered brothers come back to greet him. Şehzâde Mehmed, whom he had never met (he had died when Orhan was three), appears repeatedly, *"a young man of my own height, with kind eyes."* Şehzâde Mustafa appears at his bedside *"with the marks of the cord upon his neck, but smiling, and saying: 'Brother, do not be afraid; I forgave you long ago.'"* Most strikingly and most often, the murdered nephew **Şehzâde Murad** appears: *"He came to me again last night, as I have written before; he was a boy still of seventeen, and he sat at my bedside, and he said only: 'Uncle, I am cold,' and I awoke weeping."* It is in these letters, indeed, that we have the only direct confession from the sultan's own hand of the murder of his nephew — the confession in the form of an account of a recurring guilty dream which the sultan does not, as he might, deny:

> *"My shaykh, you ask me why this child comes to me so often; I shall tell you. I did the thing. It was a child's life that I took for a child's life, and I do not say that it was wrong, for I would do it again to keep my son alive; but the child comes, and the child says only that he is cold, and the child is right, for the dead are cold, and I am the man who made him cold."*

It is, in its single brutal couplet, perhaps the truest line ever written by an Ottoman sultan in his own hand.

There are the dreams of his father. These are the strangest, and the ones that have most exercised modern commentators. In one of the most famous, Orhan describes being summoned to the imperial tent at a place that is sometimes Mohács, sometimes Szigetvár, sometimes (impossibly) the Hippodrome; he is *stripped naked by his father's eunuchs and placed upon his father's lap, as a child*; and his father, immense and old, holds him and says *"My son, you are not yet a man; do as I say."* The sultan, in his own commentary, takes the dream as a humiliation, not as an erotic image: it is, he writes, the image of his sense that *"I shall never be half the man my father was; I shall sit upon his lap until I die, and the Padishah of the world will never grow up."*

There are, finally, the dreams that no editor of the dynastic chronicles would willingly have preserved. The sultan, in a small number of letters, confesses to recurring sexual dreams in which his wife Canan, the love of his life, betrays him with a Sudanese castrated eunuch of the harem (whose name he never gives); in some of these the dream issues in a hypothetical "next child" with Canan who is *"of a dark colour, like the date palm of Yemen."* The dreams are confessed in tones of horror and shame; the shaykh's reply, preserved in one instance, is gentle and theologically learned, interpreting them as a meditation on the sultan's own *zillet* (humiliation) before God rather than on Canan's fidelity. The dreams themselves, however, are recorded plainly. They are not the kind of thing that finds its way into the official portraiture; they have given certain modern readers (in editions that have not always been the most decorous) the occasion for some less-than-decorous jokes. They are, as the sultan himself wrote, *"my own dreams, which God has set in my own night."*

What these letters together convey is a man of immense intelligence, deep religious feeling, severe anxiety, and a great loneliness. He had no peers; his shaykhs were his teachers and his tutors, not his equals; his consorts and his children were his subjects; his viziers were his servants, his fellow-monarchs were his enemies. He was, in the literal sense of his pen-name, *Garîb* — a stranger. The dreams were the only place he could speak freely. The fact that he placed them in writing, knowing that nothing in a court ever stays entirely private, suggests that he did so partly in the hope that someone — perhaps God, perhaps posterity — would hear what he could not say to anyone in his own palace.

### Hygiene, Smell, and Appearance

The physical description of Orhan II in his maturity is consistent across a great many witnesses. He was taller than average — the bailo Donini estimated his height as *"sei piedi, una palma più alto della maggior parte dei suoi cortigiani,"* which converts to something near five feet ten inches by modern measure, well above the median for the period. He was thin to the point of slightness; the Venetian Costantino Garzoni called him *"sottile, ma non infermo; la sua snellezza è di natura e non di malattia"* ("slender, but not sickly; his slenderness is of nature and not of illness"). His hair was a chestnut brown, kept at shoulder length but rarely visible beneath the great kavuk turban of state; his beard, well-kept, was a paler shade of brown which by his last years was streaked with grey though not yet wholly so. His eyes were a clear grey. His skin was pale — pale enough to be remarked upon, and in his last years he was sometimes called *Beyaz-yüzlü*, the "white-faced" sultan. His face was not handsome by the conventions of the day — the Ottoman ideal of male beauty ran to broader, more powerful features — but it was open, expressive, and (when not closed by anxiety) frequently smiling. Garzoni's verdict — *"una faccia piacevole, più di fanciullo che d'uomo"*, "a pleasant face, more of a boy than of a man" — has been quoted by every subsequent biographer.

What was most consistently remarked upon by contemporaries, including those without access to the Topkapı, was his **smell**. The sultan was passionately, almost neurotically, hygienic. He bathed daily — twice on Fridays. His clothes were perfumed with an attar made for him alone by the imperial perfumer, a blend of Damascene rose, Yemeni frankincense, ambergris, and a small admixture of an Indian wood oil that has not been identified. The fragrance, called *itr-ı hâkânî* ("the imperial scent"), was so distinctive that the chamberlain Hâfız Mustafa Ağa recorded the sultan's habit of remaining for some moments in a room after his departure *"so that those who entered after him would carry the scent of his passage upon their clothes."* It was a sign of imperial favour to be permitted to use any portion of the same perfume, and a small bottle of it was a diplomatic gift of the very highest order; Queen Elizabeth I, in her own hand, thanked the Padishah in 1583 for *"the most precious essence we have ever received from the courts of the East, the which our perfumers cannot reproduce and which we have set aside for our own person."*

The other side of the obsession was a horror of bad smells. The sultan was reported to be unable to be in the company of a person of poor personal cleanliness. In an episode that became famous, he banished from the Topkapı in 1578 a concubine of his own household — a young Circassian girl named Nâzlı — on the grounds of an offensive personal odour, granting her a substantial dowry and arranging her marriage to an officer of the bodyguard. *"He was,"* wrote the chamberlain dryly, *"as offended by the smell of a man as some are offended by his words."*

### The Daily Routine

The most detailed account we have of the sultan's daily life in his settled years comes from a memorandum prepared by his personal physician **Hekîm Ali Çelebi** in 1584, intended for the use of his successor in the post, who was to take over the management of the sultan's increasingly delicate digestion. The document, which is preserved in the medical archives, has been quoted in part by almost every modern biographer; it deserves to be quoted in full:

> *"In the morning he rises at dawn to say his prayer for half an hour, then for another half-hour he bathes. Then he is given something pleasant as a collation, and afterwards sets himself to read and write for another hour. Then he begins to give audience to the members of the Divan on the three days of the week that this occurs. Then he goes for a walk through the garden, taking pleasure in the delight of fountains and animals for another hour, taking with him the dwarves, buffoons and others to entertain him. Then he goes back once again to studying until he considers the time for lunch has arrived. He stays at table only half an hour, and rises (to go) once again into the garden for as long as he pleases. Then he goes to say his midday prayer. Then he stops to pass the time and amuse himself with the cooks of the palace, and he will stay one or two hours with them, when it is time to say the evening prayer. Then he returns to his apartments or, if it pleases him more, he stays in the garden reading or passing the time until evening with the dwarves and buffoons, and then he returns to say his prayers, that is at nightfall. Then he dines and takes more time over dinner than over lunch, making conversation until two hours after dark, until it is time for prayer. He never fails to observe this schedule every day."*

The most striking thing about this account is its absence of imperial business. The Divan meets three days a week and consumes perhaps two hours of those days. The remainder of the sultan's time is spent in the garden, in the kitchen, in his own apartments, at prayer, and in the company of his dwarves and his buffoons. The empire is run; the orders are signed; the dispatches are read. But the sultan, by his own routine, is a private man whose business of state occupies him for the smaller part of his waking hours.

### Severity in Practice

It would be a mistake, however, to conclude from the elegance and the timidity that the sultan was incapable of severity. He was — when the occasion demanded — a man capable of harshness on a great scale, all the more frightening for its rarity. Three incidents in particular fix the point.

The first was the **massacre of the Mahdîst cult of 1580**. A heterodox movement combining Twelver Shia elements, Bektâşî millenarianism, and outright social rebellion had been active in central Anatolia since 1567, gathering peasants and small craftsmen around a charismatic preacher named Şeyh Şâh Ahmed, who proclaimed himself the *Mehdî*, the awaited messiah. The movement had killed *kâdis*, sacked tax-offices, burned *medreses* of the Sunni orthodoxy, and proved difficult to suppress for over a decade despite repeated punitive expeditions. In 1580, after a final defeat near Çorum at the hands of the *beylerbey* of Anadolu, some three thousand two hundred surviving members of the cult — including women and children of the camp — were brought in chains to Constantinople. The disposition of their fate was placed before the sultan.

Orhan, who had read the reports of the cult's atrocities (which had been substantial — at one point a *kâdi* and his family had been burned alive in their own *konak*), ordered the execution of every adult male in the company and the public display of the heads on the walls of the city. The order was carried out over three days. The chronicler Selaniki, who witnessed the procession of the carts of heads through the Divanyolu, wrote that *"the smell of blood lay upon the city for a week, and small children were taken indoors by their mothers and did not play in the streets for a month."* It was, in the long catalogue of Ottoman public severity, an unusually large massacre.

The episode, when set beside the rest of his personality, has puzzled historians. Some — particularly the harsher commentators — have taken it as evidence that the sultan's apparent gentleness was a façade and his real instincts those of his Selimid ancestors. Others, including the moderate twentieth-century biographer Necdet İlhan, have argued the opposite: that the sultan, whose deep anxiety was the threat to public order, lashed out at a movement that for thirteen years had threatened precisely the *peace* that was his obsessional priority. *"He could endure many things,"* wrote İlhan, *"but he could not endure noise; and what the Mehdîst cult had made for thirteen years was noise."*

The second was the **trap of 1584**, an episode that has been remembered ever since for what it revealed not only of the sultan but of his marriage. Two members of the inner Divan — a vizier whose name is variously given as Mehmed Paşa and Hasan Paşa in different chronicles, and a *defterdar* whose name is more securely attested as the Albanian-born Ahmed Çelebi — had attracted the sultan's suspicion through a series of small acts of perceived disloyalty, none amounting to a chargeable offence. Orhan, with his characteristic preference for indirect resolution, arranged with Canan a test.

The plan, as it was carried out in the harem on a winter evening of 1584, was theatre. Canan summoned the two suspects to a private audience — already a breach of palace convention, for these were men with no business in the harem — and proposed to them, in tones of confidential bitterness, that the sultan had become unstable; that he could no longer reign; that she had a plan to expedite his death by poisoning him and to enthrone her son Abdullah; and that she required their assistance in exchange for the most generous promotions in the new reign. Orhan was hidden behind a lattice (*kafes*) at the back of the room, listening.

The two men, by every account, agreed. They named the amount they would expect. They proposed the means. They embraced Canan and kissed her hand. They turned to leave.

The lattice opened. The sultan stepped out. He was carrying his sword — an extraordinary thing for a Padishah to do in his own harem at night, and an indication of how completely he had prepared. He cut down both men in the room without speaking; the chamber was a wreck of blood within minutes; the women of the household, hearing the noise, fled the wing; the bodyguards were summoned, and the sultan ordered the heads to be removed from the corpses.

Then — and this is the part of the episode that has fascinated commentators — he carried the two heads, holding them by the hair, through the corridors of the harem to the Hall of the Divan, where the full Divan had been summoned by the time of his arrival. He placed the heads on the great rug of state. He spoke, by the witness of three independent chamberlains' memoirs, with such ferocity that several viziers visibly trembled.

> *"This empire,"* he is recorded to have said, *"is mine. This throne is mine. This palace is mine. The ground you walk upon is mine. The air that fills your mouths when you breathe is mine. The lives you suppose to be yours, you have from me. Whoever forgets this — let him remember it now. Whoever conspires against me — let him remember it now. I am the Shadow of God upon the earth, the Padishah of the Seven Seas, the Caliph of the Faithful. Everything belongs to me. There is no piece of this that any of you can have for himself."*

He then — astonishingly — *threw* the two heads, one after another, across the chamber, like a man throwing balls at a fair. The chronicler Mustafa Âlî, who would not have invented this detail (he was not, on the whole, an inventive writer), recorded that *"the heads struck the wall of the divan and fell upon the rug, and the Padishah was breathing heavily, and his eyes were upon us, and we did not look at one another for an hour."*

He then withdrew. The bodies were disposed of by the bostancıs. The viziers returned to their houses. The episode was never spoken of in the imperial chancery, and is preserved only because three chamberlains who were present that night wrote it down for their own memoirs in the years following the reign.

What the episode reveals is the second face of the man — the face beneath the *Garîb* face, beneath the poet face, beneath the cook face. It is a face that was rarely seen but that was always there. The same man who wept before his Janissaries in 1567 could, in 1584, behead two men in his own harem and throw their heads across his Divan. He was, in the literal sense, a Padishah: a master, a lord, a man who governed by power and not only by sentiment. The error of his enemies — and of his less-careful biographers — has been to take the *Garîb* mask for the whole.

The third — slighter, but characteristic — was his treatment of **Mihrimah Sultan**, his half-sister and the most powerful surviving woman of the previous reign. Mihrimah, the only daughter of Süleyman and Hürrem, had been since her father's death the most influential single woman in the dynasty: as the *de facto valide* in the absence of a real one (Orhan having no mother), she enjoyed in the first decade of the reign a precedence at court no one else could claim. She was older than Orhan by eighteen years; she was learned, beautiful in her ageing, and politically formidable; she had her own enormous *vakıf* establishment, her own residence on the Bosporus, her own clients. Canan resented her bitterly, and Mihrimah returned the dislike with the cool unmistakable contempt of an old princess for a *jariye*. The two women never spoke directly to one another. The court existed for ten years in the careful management of their mutual avoidance.

The breach came in 1577, after Mihrimah refused to attend a small ceremony at which Canan was to be honoured publicly. The slight was unmistakable. Orhan — by every account, under sustained pressure from Canan and from her circle — issued the *fermân* banishing Mihrimah from the Topkapı. She withdrew to her own *yalı* on the Asian shore. She lived there in dignity, surrounded by her own household, for ten months. In the spring of 1578 she sickened — it is not clear with what — and died, the chronicles say, of a *kederden helâk*, "a destruction by grief." Orhan was reported to have wept at the news, to have refused food for two days, to have insisted on the burial in the *türbe* of his father in the Süleymaniye complex beside Hürrem her mother and Süleyman her father, and to have walked behind her bier on the day of the burial with his head uncovered. The reconciliation, if it was a reconciliation, was posthumous. The episode is the only one in his reign in which he is recorded to have acted, against his nature, on the sustained jealousy of his wife — and the price was the death of his last surviving sister.

## Inflation, Corruption, and the Defterdar Revolt of 1583

The economic transformation of the reign — which the chroniclers of the time did not yet have the conceptual vocabulary to describe accurately, but which has been the subject of much modern study — was the unwelcome shadow of the maritime triumphs. The vast quantities of silver flowing from the Spanish New World into the European economies were, by the 1570s, beginning to penetrate the Ottoman currency system through the Levantine trade and through the new English and Dutch commerce. The traditional silver content of the *akçe*, the small silver coin in which all wages and most taxes were paid, came under pressure as European silver flooded the markets at a price below the Ottoman mint's customary purchase price.

The Sokollu administration had managed the pressure with what skill was available; the great vizier had resisted the temptation of currency debasement until the very last years of his life, preferring instead to clamp down on the export of bullion and to maintain the *akçe*'s nominal value at the cost of a developing shortage of small coin. After his assassination in 1579 — of which more in a moment — the political will to maintain this discipline collapsed. The new grand viziers, lacking Sokollu's stature, were unable to resist the pressures from the imperial treasury for cash to pay the Persian war, the construction projects, and the maintenance of the immense imperial household. In 1580 the silver content of the *akçe* was reduced by a third; in 1582, by a further fifth.

The consequence was a sharp and rapid inflation. Prices in Constantinople — measured by the careful records of the Hagia Sophia *vakıf*, which kept its accounts in detail — roughly doubled between 1580 and 1583. The wages of soldiers, paid in *akçe* at fixed rates established when the coin had its old silver content, lost a great deal of their purchasing power. The Janissary corps, who had grumbled at the *cülûs bahşişi* in 1567, were now drawing wages that bought half as much bread as those wages had bought ten years before.

The crisis broke on the eighth of Cemâziye'l-evvel 991 (the third week of April 1583). The Janissary aghas, refusing the latest debased payroll, presented to the sultan a formal *arz* — petition — demanding the heads of the *defterdar* responsible for the debasement (the chief treasurer, the Albanian-born Mahmud Çelebi, who had become a creature of the post-Sokollu factional system) and of two of his deputies. Failing to receive a satisfactory response, the corps drew up in the second courtyard of the Topkapı in full mutinous array, broke open the gate of the imperial treasury, refused all conciliation by Canan and by Mihrimah's residual establishment, and held the palace effectively under siege for four days.

It was a worse moment for the sultan than the revolt of 1567 had been. In 1567 he had been new; he had been weeping; he had been able to plead. In 1583 he was forty-three years old, the longest-reigning sultan since the founder of the dynasty, and he had ruled in his own name for seventeen years. He could not weep before the corps and expect to be respected. What he did, instead, was send out the *defterdar* and his two deputies, who were dragged from their *konaks* by the Janissaries, strangled in the second courtyard, and their bodies thrown over the wall into the Marmara. The sultan paid out the cash in full silver from the privy treasury — which was, by 1583, sufficient — and reorganised the *defterdarlık* under a different set of officials.

The revolt was over within a week. But it changed everything. It demonstrated, to a generation of Janissaries who had not been at Belgrade in 1567, that the office of sultan was subject to the corps; that prices and wages and the silver content of the coin were the proper business of the soldiery; and that the imperial household could be made, by sufficient pressure, to deliver up its own officials. The corps remembered this lesson. Sultans of subsequent generations would remember it too, against their will and their interest. The **Defterdar Revolt of 1583** entered the political vocabulary of the empire as a precedent for every Janissary mutiny of the seventeenth century.

For Orhan personally, the revolt was the final blow. He withdrew, in its immediate aftermath, into the harem with a completeness from which he never emerged. From the summer of 1583 until his death in April 1585 he is recorded as having left the Harem buildings only for the Bayram prayers of 1584 and for two emergency audiences in the Hall of the Divan. He refused the Friday procession for thirty-two consecutive Fridays on the explicit ground — sometimes stated in his own hand, sometimes through the chamberlain — that he had received reports of plots against his life. Selaniki summarised the situation in a famous passage:

> *"The Padishah's heart was a city under siege, and he could not be persuaded that the gates were not in the act of being opened to the enemy. Whenever it was time for Friday prayer he would dress and prepare, and then he would hear a thing — a footstep, a whisper, an inadvertent laugh among the bostancıs — and he would say: 'It is not safe; not to-day; not while these things are reported in the city.' And he would put off the procession. And the people, who knew, said: 'The Padishah is not coming; the Padishah is afraid.' And it was true, and there was none who would not have done as he did, if they had been in his place."*

## The Assassination of Sokollu Mehmed Paşa (1579)

We must turn back briefly to a moment passed over above: the death, on the eve of the year of the Persian war and four years before the Defterdar Revolt, of the man whose hand had governed the empire for thirteen years.

On the fourteenth of Şâban 987 (12 October 1579), at the conclusion of the afternoon Divan meeting in the second courtyard of the Topkapı, the elderly grand vizier Sokollu Mehmed Paşa rose to leave the chamber, surrounded by his customary entourage of secretaries and bodyguards. As he passed through the doorway of the Divan a man dressed as a petitioner — bearing an *arzuhâl*, a petition, in his right hand — stepped from the line of waiting suppliants, drew a dagger from beneath his robe with his left hand, and struck the grand vizier a single blow under the right armpit. The blade pierced between the ribs and entered the lung; Sokollu fell back into the arms of his secretary; he was carried to a couch in the side chamber; he died within the hour.

The assassin — a man variously identified in the chronicles as a Bosnian dervish (which, given Sokollu's origins, was a piece of bitter Bosnian symbolism), a hired killer of the Safavid embassy, or a member of one of the Anatolian *bektâşi* networks linked to the Mehdîst movement (whose remnants were still active and had reasons to hate Sokollu) — was killed on the spot by the *çavuş*es of the Divan and his identity never definitively established. The investigation, conducted by the second vizier Lala Mustafa Paşa, produced four different reports, each accusing a different party, and the question of who had actually paid for the dagger has never been satisfactorily resolved.

The strongest contemporary suspicion fell upon the Persian embassy, then in Constantinople negotiating (a year before the war broke out) what proved to be a futile demarche over the Caucasus frontier. Sokollu was, until his death, the principal restraining voice in the Divan against the war party; with him removed, the path was clear for Lala Mustafa Paşa and Koca Sinan Paşa to push the war through. The Persian motive — to remove the only Ottoman statesman opposing the policy that was about to bring the empire into a long, ruinous, but unwanted conflict with them — was, from the Iranian point of view, perverse but not impossible. The Persians, in this reading, would have removed their best friend in the Ottoman Divan in the hope that the war could be still further deferred. There are precedents in the eastern diplomatic tradition for exactly this kind of perverse calculation.

The kinder suspicion fell upon the *Bektâşi* network of the suppressed Mehdîst cult, who had reason — Sokollu's *beylerbeyi* of Anadolu had been the most aggressive prosecutor of the cult through the 1570s — and who included Bosnian *dervish* elements that would account for the assassin's identification.

The harshest suspicion — never publicly aired during Orhan's lifetime, and largely a matter of modern speculation since — fell upon the sultan's own household. Orhan had hated and feared his grand vizier for thirteen years; he had dreamt repeatedly of being dethroned by him; he had married his niece to the Vizier to spy upon him; and within twelve months of the assassination he was at war with Persia, an outcome that suited the post-Sokollu Divan and which the dead grand vizier had alone been preventing. The case is, on the whole, weak: there is no evidence in the *Dreamworld* letters that Orhan knew of the assassination in advance (and if he had, he would, by every pattern of his self-flagellating confessional habit, almost certainly have written of it to one of his shaykhs); there is positive evidence that the immediate political consequences caught him by surprise; and his personal mourning for Sokollu — he attended the funeral, walked behind the bier, and ordered the *türbe* completed at imperial expense — was, by every observer's account, genuine. He had not loved his grand vizier; he had hated his grand vizier; he was, after his grand vizier's death, immediately and irrecoverably the worse off. *"The wall has fallen,"* he wrote to his shaykh in the days after, *"and I am in the courtyard alone."*

Whoever paid the assassin, the consequence was the same. The political balance of the reign was destroyed. The last six years of Orhan's sultanate — from October 1579 to April 1585 — are a different period from the first thirteen. They are the years of the Persian war, the Atlantic expedition, the inflation crisis, the Defterdar Revolt, the withdrawal of the sultan into his harem, and the slow disintegration of his health. They are also, not coincidentally, the years in which the *Dreamworld* letters reach their highest pitch of intensity, in which his poetry darkens further, in which his daily routine grows more eccentric, and in which the great court — formerly the workshop of an empire — becomes the antechamber of a dying man.

## The Domestic Household

### The Wife and the Confidante

Throughout his reign Orhan's emotional life was bounded by two women: Canan Sultan, the wife of his heart, and Feride Sultan, the confidante of his troubles. The two women lived in the same harem, knew the same intimate details of the same man, and detested one another with a steadiness that no diplomatic effort of the sultan ever quite managed.

**Canan Sultan** (c. 1540 – 1596) was, as we have seen, the formative female presence of the reign. She was *Haseki Sultan* from 1567; she was the legal wife of the sultan from 1569; she was the mother of six of his thirteen children, including the eldest surviving son Abdullah; she was the political confidante to whom he turned in every major decision; she was a force of her own at the court, with her own clientele, her own *vakıfs*, her own faction. She was tall, striking, deep-voiced, and possessed of a political intelligence by which she manipulated her superstitious and anxious husband on a daily basis through a network of dream-interpreters and astrologers whom she had quietly placed on her personal payroll.

A consistent feature of her management of the sultan was that she did not press the most obvious advantage of her position — namely, the elimination of his other sons in favour of her own Abdullah. She never, by every contemporary indication, whispered to him that Ali (son of the dead Saliha) or Ahmed (son of Feride) was conspiring against him. She would not have been believed — Orhan, who had read his stepmother Hürrem's career and concluded it was a guide for what not to fall victim to, would have recognised the technique at once and recoiled — and the attempt would have damaged her standing. She instead positioned Abdullah for the succession by other means: by ensuring that the boy was the eldest, by securing for him the second-best provincial sanjak (Edirne, on the European side of the straits, close to the capital), by cultivating the *kapıkulu* corps on his behalf during her husband's long years in the harem, and by patiently waiting.

She is harder to read on the question of her own feelings for the sultan. The political picture is one of cool exploitation. The personal picture is more complicated. She was at every one of her own deliveries holding his hand (which she had insisted upon, an unheard-of breach of protocol). She nursed him through his anxieties personally. She refused, in 1571 and again in 1583, to accept the offer of a separate residence at the Old Palace, although such residences were the usual lot of the *vâlide*'s rivals in waiting. When in 1574 she fell ill of a violent fever, the sultan moved into her chamber and personally fed her broth for seventeen days. When she was angered, by 1577, by his growing affection for Feride, she displayed not the cool indifference of a woman with no investment but the active, sleepless jealousy of one who had a great deal at stake; the Venetian Andrea Badoer remarked in his *relazione* of that year that *"the Haseki, who one would have thought to value only her position, has been observed of late to weep over the Padishah's attentions to her rival; this is not the appearance of policy, but of love, or of something near to love."*

Most strikingly, when she came in 1596 to her own death, after eleven years as *valide* of her son's reign, her final requests of her son were that she be buried beside Orhan in the *türbe* he had built within the Hagia Sophia, and that she be buried with the simple silver ring he had given her in Manisa in 1565 when they were young together. The two requests were granted. They speak more eloquently than anything she had said in life of the place the dead man occupied in her interior.

In a final letter she dictated to her son a few days before her death — preserved in the Süleymaniye and only published in the late twentieth century — she wrote:

> *"I looked at the icebox where they had laid him for four days, in the spring of '93 [1585]; I looked at it every time I crossed the chamber, and I could not look at it after a time, for there came such tears as I had not known myself capable of, and my heart was as a thing locked into the box with him. I have asked your father if he was lonely there. He has not answered me. I shall ask him myself in the next world. I have kept the ring, by which I shall know him again."*

She was, as I have said, a survivor. She had been taken from her village; she had been sold; she had been renamed; she had been forced into a bed she had not chosen. Within these limits, which were not negotiable, she had lived a life of formidable agency. But she had also, in her own peculiar way, loved the man whose bed she had not chosen. The historians have spent four centuries arguing about the proportions of the mixture. The mixture itself is not really in doubt.

### Feride

**Feride Sultan** (c. 1543 – 1600), the secondary affection, the Bosnian-born nurse who became the mother of Şehzâde Ahmed and of Aylin, played a different role. She was not the queen of the harem and never sought to be. She was the listener. The sultan went to her, in the late afternoons or after the evening prayer, for the kind of confession he could not make to his shaykhs, who were not in the harem; for the kind of complaint he could not make to Canan, who was too implicated in everything; for the kind of small domestic talk he had with no one else. She was patient. She remembered. She corrected him gently when he was being theatrical. The chamberlain's memoir gives a single line that has been quoted ever since: *"My lord went to the Lady Canan to be honoured, and to the Lady Feride to be understood; and he never could quite forgive either of them for being precisely what she was."*

Like Canan, Feride was not the saint that the kindly tradition has made of her. She used her sessions with the sultan to advance her own household and her own children. She corresponded with elements of the post-Sokollu Divan to her own advantage; she had at least one client among the *kazaskers*; she defended her son's place in the succession with the careful patience of a woman who knew that her son's place was the weakest of the three brothers. She is, in a famous and possibly apocryphal episode of the mid-1570s — the date is unfixed, the source is a court memoir of the next generation — alleged to have collaborated with Canan, in a rare moment of common interest, in the elimination of a third concubine whose name has been lost: a young woman of the harem who had attracted the sultan's growing attentions and who was rumoured to have conceived. The two women, by this account, arranged the woman's death by poison, and made the matter look like a sudden brain-fever. If the story is true, it is the only joint act of Canan and Feride in their twenty-five years of cohabitation in the same harem, and it indicates something about their united priorities. If it is not true — and it is not adequately corroborated — it indicates only that the men of the next generation believed of them what they were thought capable of.

Feride survived Orhan by fifteen years. She was exiled by the new *valide* Canan in 1585 — properly, banished from the Topkapı to the Old Palace, although her formal title was not stripped because of the survival of her daughter Aylin — and she was permitted by the new sultan, who was on good terms with his stepmother, to retire to a substantial *konak* in Eyüb where she lived in private dignity until her death in 1600. The sultan visited her there on a number of occasions, by the testimony of her own private secretary, *"for the same office she had performed for his late father: to listen."*

### The Children

Orhan fathered thirteen children. The list, in order of birth, runs:

1. **Naciye Sultan** (1558 – 1594) — daughter of Canan. Married twice; mother of seven; her descendants survived into the late seventeenth century.
2. **Şehzâde Abdullah** (1560 – 1603) — son of Canan; twin of Hayat. Succeeded his father as Sultan **Abdullah I**.
3. **Hayat Sultan** (1560 – 1599) — daughter of Canan; twin of Abdullah. Married a vizier; mother of three.
4. **Şehzâde Ali** (1563 – 1585) — son of Saliha Hatun, raised by Feride. Governor of Manisa from 1579. Executed by his brother Abdullah in 1585.
5. **Nur Sultan** (1564 – 1614) — daughter of Canan. Married a beylerbey; mother of four; lived the longest of any of Orhan's children.
6. **Esma Sultan** (1565 – 1597) — daughter of Canan. Married a vizier; mother of two.
7. **Şehzâde Ahmed** (1566 – 1585) — son of Feride. Governor of Bursa from 1581. Executed by his brother Abdullah in 1585.
8. **Mübeccel Sultan** (1567 – 1607) — daughter of Canan. Married twice; mother of five.
9. **Perran Sultan** (1569 – 1569) — daughter of Feride. Died at five months.
10. **Aylin Sultan** (1571 – 1632) — daughter of Feride. The longest-surviving of Orhan's children and the only non-Canan child to reach old age. Married three times; mother of nine. Her diary, written in late life, is the only direct testimony of any of Orhan's daughters to come down to us in book form.
11. **Şehzâde Adnan** (1573 – 1573) — son of Ayşe Hatun, twin of Leyla. Died in infancy.
12. **Leyla Sultan** (1573 – 1573) — daughter of Ayşe Hatun, twin of Adnan. Died in infancy.
13. **Şehzâde Alâeddin** (1575 – 1575) — son of Hediye Hatun. Died in infancy.

The pattern is striking. Of Canan's six children, none died in infancy. Of the seven children of other mothers, four died in infancy and a fifth (Saliha's son Ali) was executed at twenty-two. The contrast — six survivors out of six against three out of seven — has been remarked upon by every writer on the period, and a tradition that begins as a court rumour and ends in the seventeenth-century chronicles attributes the survival of Canan's children, and the death of the others, to her careful management of their wet-nurses, their guardians, and (it has been delicately suggested) their food. The evidence is largely conjectural. The harem was in 1573 swept by an outbreak of what was probably scarlet fever, in which Adnan and Leyla and Perran all died within a few weeks of one another; Alâeddin in 1575 died of what the household physician identified as an inflammation of the bowel. There is no contemporary evidence of poisoning. There is also, however, no contemporary investigation of those infant deaths; an Ottoman harem did not investigate the death of an infant prince in any thoroughgoing way, and the deaths of Adnan and Leyla and Perran and Alâeddin pass through the chronicles without remark, as the deaths of small children always did. The pattern is conspicuous. The cause is unknowable.

What is known is the sultan's grief. After Alâeddin's death in 1575 he refused, for the first time in his reign, to be consoled by his shaykhs, his wife, or his confidante. He wrote, in a letter to Şeyh Şabân-ı Velî dated the third of Rebî'ü'l-âhir 983 (12 July 1575):

> *"My shaykh, I have lost three children of my body within two years. I cannot make another. I do not have the heart for the making of another. Whatever I make, God takes; whatever I make, the world takes. Let me make no more sons. Let there be an end to it."*

He kept the resolution. From 1575 onwards he did not father a child. By the testimony of the *Dreamworld* letters of the years following, he had also — by perhaps 1577 — largely lost interest in sex itself: an exhaustion partly of grief, partly of age (he was thirty-seven), partly of the chronic anxiety that gnawed at all his appetites. He continued to be visited by his wife and by Feride, and the imperial bed was not unoccupied, but the children he had had were the children he had had. Of his nine surviving children at his death, seven — Naciye, Abdullah, Hayat, Nur, Esma, Mübeccel, and Aylin — would survive him. Two would not: Ali and Ahmed, his second and third sons, who would die at twenty-two and nineteen by the silken cord of their elder brother on the day of his accession.

He freed both of the women whose children died in infancy: Ayşe Hatun in 1573, after Adnan and Leyla, with a substantial dowry and her own *konak* in Galata; Hediye Hatun in 1575, after Alâeddin, on similar terms. He cut contact with both women afterwards. He had not loved them; they had borne his children; the children were dead; the women he sent away with what generosity was in his power.

### Spoiling

The children he raised in the harem — Canan's six and Feride's two surviving — he spoilt to a degree that astonished the household. The chamberlain Hâfız Mustafa Ağa wrote with regret of *"şehzâdeler ve sultanlar, çocukluklarında dahi bey-zâde gibi değil, kral gibi yetiştirildi"*, "the princes and the princesses, from their childhoods, were raised not as princely children but as petty kings." They were given their own servants from the age of four; they were excused from any of the discipline that had been the lot of Orhan's own princely childhood; they were never beaten, never made to wait, never made to apologise. They received the finest tutors, the finest music masters, the finest clothing; they were taken to gardens; they were given pets; the daughters were given dowries enormous even by the standards of the dynasty, and their weddings were celebrated with festivals to which the entire city was invited. The two surviving sons after Abdullah were promoted, in their princely careers, to provinces that no second or third son had previously held — Bursa and Manisa, both former imperial capitals, rather than the customary Konya or Amasya or Trabzon.

The fond reading of this — preserved in the family tradition and in the more sentimental of the chronicles — is that Orhan, raised motherless in the Old Palace, was determined that his own children should not know the desolation of his childhood. The harsh reading — preserved in Mustafa Âlî and in the modern academic consensus — is that he was buying them. He was a man whose entire life had been shaped by the threat of being murdered by his agnatic kin; he had himself murdered his nephew to forestall this threat; he was determined that his own children should never see him as he had been forced to see his half-brothers, as the obstacle to their own survival. He spoilt them so that they would love him; he spoilt them so that they would not turn against him. The strategy worked in his lifetime — there is no recorded instance of any of his children ever taking against him — but it had certain consequences after his death, when the spoilt brood found themselves out of the protection of the only person who had ever indulged them.

His daughters, in particular, were a difficulty. Naciye was wilful and unkind to her servants; Hayat was vain to a degree that was the talk of the bailo's reports; Esma intervened, against all custom and all propriety, in the affairs of her husband's *eyalet*; Nur conducted what was widely supposed to be an affair with one of her own black eunuchs and had to be sent for two years to the *yalı* at Üsküdar to extinguish the rumour. Aylin alone, the daughter of Feride, was by all reports a model of the imperial princess; she was also, in her old age, the only one of Orhan's children to write candidly of her family in her own hand. Of her sisters Aylin wrote, in her diary of the 1620s: *"They were not bad women. They had been raised to expect that the world would do as they wished, and when the world did not, they did not have the strength of soul to learn it."*

His three sons after Abdullah — and the question of his other two sons after Abdullah is the question that broke them in 1585 — had their own difficulties.

**Şehzâde Ali**, the son of Saliha, was the second-born; he was raised by Feride; he was a handsome, headstrong, athletic boy who grew into a handsome, headstrong, athletic man. He was sent to Manisa as *sancakbeyi* in 1579 at sixteen. His governorship was, by every report that reached the capital, troubling. He was unstable in his moods; he was prodigally generous to favourites and savagely punitive to their enemies; he was given to outbursts of theatrical violence that were not, by the standards of the dynasty, easily explained. In one incident that found its way into the dispatches of 1582, he had a junior *kâdı* of the province who had displeased him over a property judgment hanged from the gate of his own court; the dispatches reported, dryly, that *"the şehzâde was thereafter heard to ask after his health, and to be displeased to learn of his death."* The chamberlain's memoir is candid: *"Şehzâde Ali was a tyrant in his disposition, and he enjoyed the office of tyranny for its own sake; one does not say so of a son of one's late master, but one writes it for the future."*

**Şehzâde Ahmed**, the son of Feride, was the third-born; he was raised by his mother; he was a clever, slight, secretive boy who grew into a clever, slight, secretive man. He was sent to Bursa as *sancakbeyi* in 1581 at fifteen. His governorship was, on the political side, blameless; on the personal side, scandalous. The scandal was simply put: Ahmed enjoyed sexual relations with the wives of other men, and he preferred his partners to be unwilling. The reports of his behaviour reached the capital from the third year of his governorship, and they were the kind of reports that the *kâdı* of Bursa, an old man who was no friend of the imperial household, sent in fully documented form. The court suppressed the reports; the cases of the affected women were settled out of the privy purse; the *kâdı* was retired with a generous pension. But Ahmed was, by 1584, a man with a name in the city; and his name was not forgotten on the day, the next year, when his elder brother sent the silken cord to fetch him.

**Abdullah** himself — the heir, the firstborn surviving son, the apple of his mother's eye — is the third figure who must be mentioned here, although I shall say only what is necessary for the present narrative. He was sent to Edirne as *sancakbeyi* in 1576, at sixteen — the youngest of the three to be appointed, and to the closest of the three provincial seats. His princely household was managed by tutors of the highest quality, by an entourage carefully selected by his mother's network, and by a personal *lala* whose name will recur in the events of 1585. He was, by the report of every observer who saw him in his princely years, his mother's son: tall, deep-voiced, careful in his manner, devout in his observance, and capable — when it mattered — of acts of decision that astonished those who had thought him passive. He was also, in the assessment of every Venetian bailo who had occasion to meet him, intellectually unremarkable. Andrea Badoer, in a despairing dispatch of 1582, called him *"un giovine di buona presenza ma di mediocre ingegno, formato interamente nella corte di sua madre e non altrove."* It was an unkind assessment, and it was not entirely fair; it was also not entirely wrong. The mother had made the son the politician she needed him to be, and the politician — when his hour came — performed.

## The Final Years (1583–1585)

### The Closed Door

From the summer of 1583, after the Defterdar Revolt, the sultan effectively closed his door. He had been a sedentary man for fifteen years; he was now, in the last twenty-two months of his life, a confined one. The chronicle of these months is, paradoxically, less rich than that of the years preceding, because so much of the public life of the empire took place outside his presence. The Divan continued to meet; orders went out; campaigns continued in Persia (where 1583 was the year of the great victory of the Torches and 1584 a year of consolidation); ambassadors came and went; the Şehnâme studio in the Topkapı continued its slow production of forgeries. The sultan himself read, prayed, wrote, gardened (within the harem's walled courts), and increasingly slept badly.

His physician's notes from this period are vivid. The sultan was eating less; he was complaining of dyspepsia; he was sleeping in short bursts of two and three hours; his weight, never great, was visibly less; he was nervous to the point of being startled by the closing of a door. Hekîm Ali Çelebi tried various remedies — a mild infusion of opium-poppy for sleep; a tincture of pomegranate skin for the digestion; the regular bathing routine maintained; the diet simplified — and the sultan was a docile patient, but the underlying condition (which the physician identified, with the limited diagnostic tools of his day, as *vesvese-i şedide*, "violent obsession") did not improve.

Through it all the sultan continued his correspondence with his shaykhs. The last *Dreamworld* letter is dated the twenty-third of Cemâziye'l-evvel 993 (the second week of March 1585), three weeks before his death. It is short. It is addressed to Şeyh Mahmûd Hüdâyî. It reads, in its entirety:

> *"My shaykh, I dreamt last night a thing I have not dreamt before. I dreamt that I was a small bird in the bough of a tree, and the wind came, and the bough moved, and the bough was a strong bough, and I knew that I could not fall, and there was no cold. I have not had such a dream in nineteen years. Pray for me. Garîb."*

It was, by every reading of those who knew him, the dream of a man preparing for his end.

### The Salutation

The famous episode at the end of his life — recorded by both Selaniki and the chamberlain Hâfız Mustafa Ağa — was the salutation of the four caliphs. On the fifth of April 1585, the day before his death, the sultan was sitting in his apartment in the harem in the early afternoon. He had not been well for the previous three days, complaining of a sharp pain in the lower right quadrant of his abdomen — the symptoms, retrospectively, of a perforated appendix. The physicians had been summoned and had been able to do nothing, the condition being entirely beyond the surgical practice of the day. The pain came and went. The sultan was in a half-feverish state, drowsy and reading.

A servant — a young palace page named, in Hâfız Mustafa's account, Mehmed Çelebî — entered the chamber to remove a tray. As he was leaving, he heard the sultan say, in a clear voice, *"Aleykümü's-selâm."* Then again. Then twice more, four times in all: *"Aleykümü's-selâm. Aleykümü's-selâm. Aleykümü's-selâm. Aleykümü's-selâm."*

The page, supposing that someone had entered the chamber, looked around. The chamber was empty except for the sultan and himself.

He approached the bed. *"My Padishah,"* he said, *"there is no one in the room. To whom did you reply?"*

The sultan looked at him. He smiled slightly. *"Mehmed,"* he said, *"do not be afraid. Ebû Bekir was the first; then Ömer; then Osmân; then Alî. They came in turn, and they greeted me. They said that I shall ascend before our Prophet to-morrow."*

He turned his face. He slept.

The next afternoon, on the sixth of April 1585 — a Friday, by every account, of unusual storminess: a heavy rain falling all morning, the wind from the Black Sea so strong that the *kayık*s could not cross the Bosphorus, the sky a continuous slate-grey — the sultan's appendix ruptured. He died, in agony for the last hour of his life and with the recitation of the *kelime-i şehâdet* on his lips, shortly after the call to the afternoon prayer. He was forty-five years old. He had reigned for eighteen years, six months, and twenty-nine days.

## The Concealment and the Succession

The events that followed his death are among the most extraordinary in the dynastic history of the empire, and they belong properly to the reign that succeeded his rather than to his own. They must be sketched briefly here, because they were not the work of the dead sultan but of his wife, and because they shaped the dispensation of his family and of his memory.

**Canan Sultan**, on hearing of her husband's death, did three things in rapid succession. She summoned the grand vizier (then the elderly Koca Sinan Paşa, a creature of her connection), and the chief black eunuch of the harem (her personal client), and informed them. She summoned her son's *çavuş*es and dispatched her fastest riders, on the imperial post, with sealed letters to Edirne for Abdullah. And she ordered the sultan's body removed — not to the *mosque* for the ritual washing, which would have required the proclamation of his death — but to the great icebox of the imperial kitchens, where the winter ice was stored to make sherbets and ice for the household through the summer. The body was wrapped in cold cloths, laid in the icebox, and the box was locked.

For four days, the sultan was not dead. The Divan met; the grand vizier signed orders in the sultan's name (with seals to which Canan had access); the daily routine of the palace was maintained; the chamberlains carried trays of food into the empty apartment and carried them out again. The deception was the second of its kind in the dynasty's history — the first having been Sokollu's, eighteen years before, at Belgrade — and was executed with similar coolness.

The reason was the reason that had governed Canan's politics for thirty years. She wanted Abdullah to reach the capital before either of his brothers heard the news. Abdullah, at Edirne, was the closest of the three. The fastest route from the capital to Edirne was three days, hard riding. The fastest route from Manisa was six days; from Bursa, four. By the time the post-rider from her household reached Abdullah, the prince was three days from the capital; by the time any rumour leaked outside the Topkapı, no rider from the official channels would have an advantage. It worked. Abdullah received the news on the morning of the eighth of April, mounted within the hour, reached the *üsküdar* shore by the eleventh, and crossed to the European side at the imperial *kayık* on the morning of the twelfth. He entered the Topkapı in the early afternoon. The death of his father was, that evening, publicly announced. The funeral was held the following day. The *cülûs* of Sultan Abdullah I, with the *biât* of the Divan in the second courtyard, followed immediately.

His brothers, Ali at Manisa and Ahmed at Bursa, learned of their father's death only with the public proclamation. By the time they could ride, the throne was taken. Both were summoned to the capital under the appearance of pilgrimage to their father's funeral. Both came. Both were strangled on the evening of their arrival, by separate teams of *dilsiz* executioners, on their brother's order; their bodies were placed in coffins prepared for the purpose; they were buried, with the appearance of full imperial honours, in the *türbe* their father had built for himself in the Hagia Sophia. Ali was twenty-two; Ahmed was nineteen. Ali had a son, the infant **Şehzâde Korkut**, then two years old, who had been brought from Manisa with his mother; the boy was strangled in the harem in the same week, on Canan's personal order, against — by all reports — the explicit reluctance of the new sultan, who had not wished to extinguish a child. The story of how Canan obtained Abdullah's seal for the warrant and signed it herself, without his knowledge, and how Abdullah on discovering the act *thanked* his mother for taking the burden from his hands, belongs to the reign that followed. It cannot be developed here.

Canan herself did, in the days after the burial, one act that belongs properly to this account. She ordered that the body of her husband — which had been transferred from the icebox to the *gusülhâne*, washed and prepared in the ordinary way — be buried not in the Süleymaniye next to his father, as had been the assumption of the palace, but in a small *türbe* she ordered the architect Davud Ağa (Sinan's pupil and successor) to construct as a free-standing structure in the courtyard of the Hagia Sophia. The choice was extraordinary. The Hagia Sophia was the great Friday mosque of the imperial line, the conquest of Mehmed II, the symbol of the Ottoman conversion of Constantinople; no sultan had been buried there before. The structure was small, hexagonal, of plain Marmara stone, with a low dome and a marble *sandûka* at its centre. The work was completed in 1586. The sultan was buried in it on the day of his funeral, in temporary state; he was moved into the completed *türbe* the next year. Canan herself, eleven years later, was buried beside him.

Why she chose the Hagia Sophia, in defiance of every dynastic custom, is not certainly known. The most plausible explanation, advanced by the Ottoman antiquarian Evliya Çelebi in the next century, was that she wished her husband to be remembered not as the son of Süleyman — whose long shadow he had spent his life beneath — but as a Padishah in his own right, at the symbolic centre of the Ottoman conquest; that she wished him to be honoured by every prayer offered in the great mosque, by every visitor from the Christian and Muslim worlds who would pass through it, by every Friday hutba which carried the imperial titulature for the next centuries. It was the gesture of a woman who, after thirty years at his side, would not let the world consign him to his father's shadow. It was also, characteristically, a political act: it set the precedent for her own burial in the same *türbe*, which was the precedent she had wished to set.

## Legacy and Historiography

The reign of Orhan II has been the subject of as much contention as that of any sovereign of his dynasty, and the contention has been, on the whole, unkind. He has been treated by the chroniclers, the polemicists, the historians of the empire, and the modern enthusiasts of the dynasty's history as the man who broke the spell of the great age. He has been called the Weak, the Cowardly, the Indifferent, the Hidden, the Lazy, the Useless, the End of the Beginning. The list of his epithets in his own lifetime — *Zaîf*, *Aşçı*, *Garîb* — is mild beside the catalogue accumulated in the centuries after. He has been treated, in the popular tradition both within and outside the empire, as the *first* of the bad sultans, the first generation that fell away from the standard of the founders, the man whose failures of will and of public dignity set the dynasty on the long road to its decline.

This is partly the verdict of his contemporaries and partly the inertia of historiographic tradition. The most influential of the early chronicles — Selaniki, Mustafa Âlî, and Peçevî — were written by men with little to thank Orhan for. Selaniki, the most balanced of the three, gave him a generally fair hearing; Mustafa Âlî, who had been a junior secretary in the Sokollu network and had hoped for advancement under the Sokollu sons in the next generation, never forgave Orhan for the way he had treated the dead Bosnian; Peçevî, writing fifty years later from a generation that had inherited the inflation and the Janissary indiscipline of the late Orhan period, blamed the visible decline of his own time on the precedents that had been set in the 1580s. The judgment that Orhan was the man who ruined the empire was substantially Peçevî's, and it has been borrowed by every subsequent writer of the orthodox dynastic tradition.

What is left out of that judgment is what was actually accomplished in his reign. Cyprus. Tunis. Morocco. The Canaries. Socotra. Yemen. Hormuz. Mogadishu. The Maldives. Aceh. The patronage of Mimar Sinan. The Orhâniye complex. The marble of the Ka'ba. The *minbar* of the Prophet's Mosque. The Anglo-Ottoman partnership. The defeat of Russia. The poisoning of Tahmâsp. The patronage of the coffeehouse. The patronage of the doner kebab. The *Şehnâme*, in its final corrected form, which is among the great manuscripts of the world. The *dîvân* of Garîb, which is among the most personal poetic achievements of the Ottoman literary tradition. The *Dreamworld* letters, which are the most candid private testimony left to us by any wearer of the *tâc-ı şâhî*.

It is also worth noting what did not happen in his reign. There was no civil war among his brothers, because his brothers were dead. There was no rebellion of a son, because his sons did not rebel against him. There was no humiliating military defeat of the empire, despite the failures at Sicily and Malta and in the Americas, because the strategic balance was maintained. The Habsburgs were contained. The Persians were eventually defeated. The Russians were broken for a generation. The frontier of the empire, on his death, was vastly larger than on his accession. The treasury, despite the inflation, was full enough to pay the *cülûs bahşişi* of his successor with no debasement at all. The empire he handed to his son, for all its accumulated problems, was the empire at its imperial high noon.

He was not a great sultan in the manner of his father. He was not a great soldier; he was not a great administrator; he was not a great mover of men. He did not lead, in the way that his ancestors had led. But he occupied the office during a period of unprecedented expansion which he had the wit to allow the men around him to accomplish; he patronised the arts with a personal sensibility unique in the dynasty; and he preserved the imperial settlement, in its essentials, through a transitional generation that other dynasties have not survived. He paid a personal price in psychological terror for the office he had not sought. He died, at forty-five, of an illness that no physician of his day could cure. He left his wife and his son a working empire. They threw away parts of it; that was their business; it was not, in retrospect, his.

The harsher historical judgment of him, summarised in the popular phrase *"if Mustafa had lived, the empire would still stand,"* is sentimental and counterfactual and, on close inspection, false. Mustafa would have inherited the same fiscal pressures and the same geopolitical strains and the same Janissary corps and the same European silver. The decisions Mustafa would have made are unknowable; the assumption that he would have made better ones is the assumption of those for whom the cult of personality has replaced the analysis of structure. The reign of Orhan II was the reign that actually occurred; it was the reign in which the empire achieved its greatest geographic extent and its richest cultural moment; and it was the reign of a man who, by every personal index, suffered for his office.

He had asked, in one of his earliest letters to his shaykh, to be permitted to lay down his head and walk away, *"so that no one would look for me, and I should find peace."* The wish was not granted. He kept the head, and the head wore the *tâc*, and the *tâc* was heavy. He bore it for nineteen years. He bore it badly, and he bore it well; he bore it as much as a man of his particular constitution could bear it; he did not throw it away. When he was done, he passed it to his son, and the storm outside the windows of the Topkapı broke into rain.

> *Garîb'im ben bu cihanda, garîb yatar gönlüm,
> Allâh'ım, sen bilirsin, ne ararsın bu kuldan, ne bu kul senden niyâz.*

> ("I am a stranger in this world, and a stranger my heart lies; / my God, you know what you seek from this slave, and what this slave begs of you.")

— Garîb, *Dîvân*, gazel VII, ll. 11–12.

## Tughra

The *tughra* of Orhan II, designed at his accession by the head of the imperial chancery, reads in full:

> *Orhan Şâh ibn Süleymân Şâh el-muzaffer dâimâ*
> ("Orhan Shah, son of Süleyman Shah, ever victorious")

It is recognisable from those of his father by its slightly narrower vertical stems and by the inclusion of a small marginal motif of a six-petalled rosette — a personal addition, by tradition imposed by the sultan himself, intended to represent the six children he had had by Canan.
