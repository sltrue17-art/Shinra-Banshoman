Mighty sovereigns of the Ottoman throne: Sultan Orhan II

The eleventh sultan turned his face from the saddle to the sea, and reigned over an age in which Ottoman banners were unfurled from the Canaries to the shores of the Indies

By Daily Sabah | ISTANBUL | Published 09.06.2026 · 5 min read
f𝕏✆✉

When Sultan Orhan II was girded with the sword of Osman in 1566, succeeding his father, Suleiman the Magnificent, he inherited an empire that spanned three continents and stood at the very summit of its glory. He would reign for 19 years as the 11th Ottoman sultan and third caliph of the dynasty — and in that span he became the first sovereign of the House of Osman to govern wholly from his capital, never once riding out at the head of his army.

Yet the reign of this quiet, devout and learned ruler coincided with one of the most dazzling chapters in the empire's history. So brilliant was the fortune of his fleets upon the waters that the sultan was acclaimed with a title borne by no padishah before him: "Pâdişâh-ı Heft Deniz," the Padishah of the Seven Seas.

A prince of Trabzon

Şehzade Orhan was born on Jan. 3, 1540, in the Old Palace (Eski Saray) of Istanbul, the youngest of the surviving sons of Suleiman the Magnificent. His mother, Havva Hatun, died while he was still an infant, and the prince was raised within the imperial household and tutored in the Quran, in the Arabic and Persian tongues, in music and in the arts of the pen, for which he showed an early and lasting love.

As was the custom for an Ottoman prince, he was sent in his youth to govern in the provinces, holding the sanjak of Trabzon and later that of Manisa. It was in these years that he took into his household the consort who would remain his companion for the rest of his life — Canan Sultan, a lady of grace and intelligence whom he would later free and wed as his lawful wife, and who would in time rise to become one of the most influential figures of the imperial harem.

Accession

Suleiman the Magnificent breathed his last in 1566 in his campaign tent before the fortress of Szigetvár, far from his capital and surrounded by the marching army. The grand vizier, Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, kept the sultan's death secret and guided the host homeward, and it was at Belgrade that the soldiery at last learned of their master's passing and swore the oath of allegiance to his heir. Orhan, who had been left as regent in Istanbul, thus ascended the throne, and the long, settled reign of a sovereign of the pen and the sea began.

The Padishah of the Seven Seas

Orhan II entrusted the conduct of war to the celebrated Sokollu Mehmed Pasha and to a generation of gifted admirals, and under their banners the empire turned decisively toward the oceans. Cyprus was taken, and then Crete; the conquest of the Aegean islands was at last completed. In the south the islands of the Red Sea and distant Socotra were brought under the sultan's authority, together with the pearl-rich coasts of the Gulf — Bahrain, Lahsa and the harbors of Oman — while in the Persian Gulf his captains contested the Strait of Hormuz with the Portuguese.

In the west, after the final reduction of Tunis, an Ottoman army answered the appeal of the Muslims of the Maghreb al-Aqsa and crossed into Morocco, where it shattered a great Iberian invasion — the king of Portugal himself fell upon that field — and the Maghreb entered the empire as a province. From there the sultan's forces seized the Canary Islands, and with that conquest the Ottoman state ceased to be a power of the Mediterranean alone and became a power of the open Atlantic.

The Friday sermon was henceforth read and coin was struck in Orhan's name from the Canaries in the west to the islands of the Indies in the east. The sultanate of Aceh and the Maldives sought his protection against the Portuguese; on the Swahili coast of East Africa his suzerainty was acknowledged at Mogadishu, Barawa, Mombasa and Kilwa. There were checks, too — Sicily, Malta and the Balearic Islands withstood every assault — but the wealth of two oceans now flowed into the imperial treasury, and the age was rightly remembered as a golden one upon the seas.

From Fez to London

Nor did the sultan neglect the affairs of the continents. In 1578 a great war was opened against Safavid Persia in the east, a struggle that would outlast his own reign; and in the north his Crimean vassals carried Ottoman arms to the very approaches of Moscow, compelling the tsar to sue for terms. In the west the old friendship with France was preserved, the Protestant Dutch looked to the sultan in their long quarrel with Spain, and a remarkable correspondence flowered between Istanbul and the court of Queen Elizabeth I of England, the two rulers exchanging warm letters and rich gifts.

"To the most sublime and powerful ruler of the world, the protector and sole sovereign of the Islamic faith ... I offer our friendship and beg for your protection, so that our subjects may trade safely within your territories." — Queen Elizabeth I to Sultan Orhan II, 1582

The pen, the tile and the table

Orhan II was, above all, a builder and a patron. He set the great court architect Mimar Sinan to raise the Orhaniye Complex at Trabzon between 1572 and 1576 — a mosque, a madrasah, a hospital and a soup kitchen (imaret) — which remains to this day the most visited monument of that city. In the Holy Cities his piety was lavish: he endowed a madrasah, a school, a lodge and a great imaret at Mecca and Medina, clad the walls of the Kaaba in marble, and in 1581 sent from Istanbul the carved marble minbar that still stands in the Prophet's Mosque.

A lover of letters, he composed poetry under the pen name "Garib," verses praised for their tender and melancholy grace, and he was a musician who set his own compositions. Under his patronage the art of the Ottoman miniature reached new heights, crowned by the magnificent illustrated chronicle of his reign, the "Şehname-i Sultan Orhan." He gathered historians to his court so that the deeds of the empire might be recorded by its own hand rather than by foreigners. He was famous, too, as a cook of rare skill — the people fondly named him "Aşçı," the Cook — and he opened coffeehouses for the enjoyment of his subjects.

The nature of the sultan

Contemporaries describe Orhan II as a devout and intellectual sovereign, drawn to mysticism, to astronomy and to history, and forever in the company of scholars. He was tender-hearted and open-handed, slow to refuse a petitioner, and possessed of a ready good humor that endeared him to those about him. His magnanimity toward the wider dynasty was much remarked: he restored the honor and the fortune of Mahidevran Sultan, the mother of the late Şehzade Mustafa, and raised a worthy tomb for that beloved prince.

He was a sedentary ruler who seldom left his palace, and some among his contemporaries faulted him for never taking the field as his ancestors had done. Yet his realm enjoyed an unbroken stability and a plenty seldom equaled, and the sultan governed it with a quiet, careful hand to the end of his days.

Death and legacy

Sultan Orhan II died on April 8, 1585, at Topkapı Palace, carried off by a sudden illness at the age of 45, after a reign of 19 years. He was laid to rest in the tomb (türbe) he had himself commissioned in the courtyard of the Hagia Sophia. He was succeeded by his son, Sultan Abdullah, born of his beloved Canan Sultan, who would herself rise to become one of the most powerful women of the age as Valide Sultan.

History remembers Orhan II as a paradox among the padishahs — a sovereign who never drew a sword in battle, yet under whom Ottoman power first became truly oceanic; the poet Garib, the cook upon the throne, and the Padishah of the Seven Seas.
